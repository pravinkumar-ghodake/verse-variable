## 1. Purpose

Verse & Variable is committed to maintaining a literary and intellectual archive that values accuracy, transparency, responsible attribution, and continuing improvement.

Despite careful research and editorial review, published material may occasionally contain errors, incomplete information, outdated references, or interpretations that require reconsideration.

This policy explains how readers, researchers, authors, translators, contributors, and other interested individuals can report concerns about published material and how those concerns may be evaluated.

The policy applies to all publicly available content, including:

- Individual literary explorations.
- Original literary quotations.
- Translations and transliterations.
- Historical and cultural commentary.
- Philosophical discussions.
- Scientific and technical explanations.
- Bibliographic references.
- Images, illustrations, and multimedia.
- Article metadata and attribution.
- Editorial and informational pages.

The objective is to maintain the reliability of the archive while preserving its intellectual independence and interpretative character.

## 2. Fundamental Principles

### 2.1 Accuracy

Published factual claims should be supported by appropriate evidence.

Where an error is identified, the publication should make a reasonable effort to investigate and correct it.

### 2.2 Transparency

Material corrections should be handled transparently.

Readers should not be misled into believing that an important factual or scientific claim has remained unchanged when it has been substantially corrected.

### 2.3 Evidence-Based Review

Corrections should be assessed according to the quality, relevance, and reliability of the supporting evidence.

The identity, prominence, institutional position, or personal opinion of the person submitting a correction should not determine the outcome.

### 2.4 Editorial Independence

The publication retains responsibility for its editorial decisions.

A correction request does not automatically require a change to an article.

Legitimate disagreement, alternative literary interpretation, and scholarly debate should remain possible.

### 2.5 Respectful Engagement

Correction requests should be considered respectfully, including when they challenge an important part of an existing article or require reconsideration of an earlier editorial decision.

### 2.6 Continuous Improvement

The purpose of corrections is not merely to remove mistakes. It is also to improve the accuracy, clarity, traceability, and long-term usefulness of the archive.

## 3. What Can Be Reported?

Readers may report concerns involving any aspect of the publication.

### 3.1 Literary Textual Errors

Examples include:

- Incorrect words or characters in an original poem.
- Missing or additional lines.
- Incorrect couplet or stanza order.
- Incorrect punctuation where it changes the meaning.
- Confusion between different editions.
- Incorrectly combined textual variants.
- Mistakes introduced during digital transcription.

The original text should be checked against a reliable source whenever possible.

### 3.2 Authorship and Attribution Errors

Examples include:

- A poem attributed to the wrong author.
- An incorrect lyricist or composer.
- Confusion between a singer and the original writer.
- Incorrect performer attribution.
- An unidentified or incorrectly credited translator.
- A disputed attribution presented as established fact.
- Incorrect publication or archival information.

Attribution concerns should be supported by reliable bibliographic, archival, historical, or other relevant evidence.

### 3.3 Translation and Transliteration Errors

Examples include:

- Incorrect translation of a word or expression.
- Loss of a meaningful ambiguity.
- Incorrect transliteration.
- Misinterpretation of an idiom.
- Incorrect grammatical or cultural explanation.
- Unacknowledged alterations to an existing translation.
- Failure to distinguish an adaptation from a close translation.

Translation disagreements should be examined in the context of the original language and the literary expression.

Different defensible translations do not automatically constitute errors.

### 3.4 Historical and Cultural Errors

Examples include:

- Incorrect historical dates.
- Inaccurate biographical information.
- Misrepresented literary traditions.
- Incorrect geographical or cultural references.
- Unsupported statements about historical events.
- Misattribution of philosophical or cultural ideas.

Claims should be assessed using appropriate historical, scholarly, archival, or other reliable sources.

### 3.5 Philosophical and Interpretative Concerns

Readers may report:

- An inaccurate description of a philosophical position.
- Incorrect attribution of an idea to a thinker.
- Misrepresentation of a philosophical tradition.
- Unsupported claims about historical influence.
- A misleading interpretation of a cited text.

However, literary and philosophical interpretations can legitimately differ.

An alternative interpretation should not automatically be treated as proof that the existing article is factually incorrect.

### 3.6 Scientific and Technical Errors

Examples include:

- Incorrect equations.
- Incorrect scientific definitions.
- Misuse of technical terminology.
- Incorrect units or dimensions.
- Mathematical or computational errors.
- Misrepresentation of experimental findings.
- Treating a numerical simulation as experimental validation.
- Incorrect descriptions of nonlinear mechanisms.
- Unsupported claims about material behaviour.
- Misinterpretation of scientific publications.
- Outdated technical references.

Scientific concerns should be examined against appropriate research, mathematical formulations, experimental evidence, or authoritative technical sources.

### 3.7 Citation and Reference Errors

Examples include:

- Incorrect DOI.
- Broken links.
- Incorrect publication details.
- Missing author information.
- A quotation that cannot be located in the cited source.
- A source that does not support the associated claim.
- A retracted or corrected publication presented without qualification.

A citation should support the specific statement for which it is provided.

### 3.8 Copyright and Intellectual Property Concerns

Reports may concern:

- Reproduction without appropriate permission.
- Incorrect copyright ownership.
- Misattributed translations.
- Unauthorised photographs or illustrations.
- Reproduction of protected lyrics.
- Incorrect licence information.
- Missing required attribution.
- Other intellectual property concerns.

These reports should be reviewed separately from ordinary editorial corrections when necessary.

### 3.9 Privacy and Confidentiality Concerns

Readers may also report:

- Unintended disclosure of personal information.
- Incorrect identification of an individual.
- Exposure of confidential information.
- Sensitive information appearing in an article, image, or document.
- Other significant privacy concerns.

Such matters should receive appropriate consideration and may justify temporary restriction of affected material while the concern is reviewed.

### 3.10 Technical and Accessibility Errors

Examples include:

- Broken article links.
- Incorrect language metadata.
- Urdu direction or rendering problems.
- Missing image descriptions.
- Incorrect article titles.
- Broken weekly collection relationships.
- Inaccessible navigation.
- Formatting problems.
- Incorrect structured metadata.
- Search or archive indexing errors.

## 4. What Is Not Necessarily an Error?

Not every disagreement requires a correction.

The publication recognises the distinction between factual accuracy and interpretative diversity.

The following do not automatically constitute grounds for changing an article.

### 4.1 Alternative Literary Interpretation

Readers may interpret a poem, ghazal, song, or literary passage differently.

A different reading does not automatically invalidate the published interpretation.

### 4.2 Different Translation Choices

A single literary expression may support several defensible translations.

A correction is more appropriate when a translation materially misrepresents the source, introduces an unsupported meaning, or contains a verifiable linguistic error.

### 4.3 Personal Preferences

Disagreement with an article's tone, style, selection, or editorial perspective does not automatically establish factual inaccuracy.

Constructive criticism is welcome, but editorial preferences and factual corrections are distinct matters.

### 4.4 Good-Faith Intellectual Disagreement

Readers may disagree about philosophical positions, scientific interpretations, historical significance, or interdisciplinary comparisons.

Such disagreements should be examined on their merits.

An article may retain a clearly identified interpretative position while acknowledging credible alternative explanations.

### 4.5 Unsupported Assertions

A request to remove or change material should provide sufficient context for meaningful evaluation.

An unsupported demand does not automatically require revision.

The absence of adequate evidence does not necessarily establish that a published statement is correct; it may simply mean that further verification is required.

## 5. How to Submit a Correction

Correction requests may be submitted through the editor's official website:

https://sites.google.com/view/pravinkumarghodake

When submitting a concern, provide enough information to identify and evaluate it.

### 5.1 Recommended Information

A useful correction request should contain:

**1. Article identification**

- Article title.
- Permanent article URL.
- Relevant section or paragraph.

**2. Nature of the concern**

Identify whether the issue concerns:

- Original literary text.
- Authorship or attribution.
- Translation or transliteration.
- Historical or cultural information.
- Philosophical interpretation.
- Scientific or technical accuracy.
- Citation or reference.
- Copyright or permission.
- Privacy.
- Accessibility or technical presentation.

**3. Existing statement**

Quote or identify the specific statement that appears incorrect.

**4. Proposed correction**

Explain what should be changed and why.

**5. Supporting evidence**

Where possible, provide:

- Original source.
- Book or edition information.
- Page or section number.
- DOI.
- Academic publication.
- Archival reference.
- Official documentation.
- Reliable supporting URL.

**6. Additional context**

Explain any relevant uncertainty, competing source, alternative interpretation, or methodological limitation.

### 5.2 Supporting Documents

Relevant documents or references may help establish the concern.

However, do not submit passwords, government identification numbers, financial information, confidential manuscripts, or unrelated sensitive personal information through ordinary communication channels.

### 5.3 Anonymity and Identification

A person may communicate a correction without requesting public acknowledgement.

Submitting feedback does not automatically authorise the publication to identify the person publicly.

Requests involving confidential correspondence or sensitive information should explain any relevant confidentiality concerns.

## 6. Correction Review Process

Every substantive correction request should be assessed according to its nature and significance.

The review may involve the following stages.

### Stage 1 — Identification

Identify the affected article, page, statement, or resource.

Determine whether the concern relates to a factual error, interpretation, attribution, rights issue, accessibility problem, or technical defect.

### Stage 2 — Initial Assessment

Assess whether the concern is sufficiently specific to investigate.

Where essential information is missing, additional clarification or supporting references may be requested.

### Stage 3 — Source Examination

Review the original source and relevant supporting material.

Depending on the issue, this may involve:

- Comparing literary editions.
- Examining original-language text.
- Reviewing translation sources.
- Checking archival records.
- Consulting scholarly literature.
- Verifying mathematical formulations.
- Examining experimental or numerical evidence.
- Reviewing copyright and licence information.
- Testing the affected technical functionality.

### Stage 4 — Evaluation

Determine whether the available evidence supports:

- A factual error.
- A partial error.
- An incomplete explanation.
- An alternative interpretation.
- A genuine unresolved dispute.
- No identifiable error.
- The need for additional investigation.

### Stage 5 — Editorial Decision

Decide whether the issue requires:

- A minor correction.
- A substantive correction.
- A clarification.
- An additional reference.
- A qualified statement.
- A visible correction notice.
- Temporary restriction.
- Replacement or withdrawal.
- No change.

### Stage 6 — Implementation

Where a correction is accepted, update the affected material and its associated metadata or references.

Check whether the change also affects the weekly collection, sitemap, search index, structured metadata, or other linked resources.

### Stage 7 — Documentation

Record significant changes in accordance with the publication's correction and version-history practices.

The review process should remain proportionate to the seriousness and complexity of the issue.

## 7. Correction Categories

Corrections should be classified according to their significance.

### 7.1 Minor Corrections

These include:

- Spelling mistakes.
- Typographical errors.
- Minor punctuation corrections.
- Formatting adjustments.
- Broken links.
- Non-substantive metadata corrections.

Minor corrections may be implemented without a prominent public correction notice when the change does not materially affect the reader's understanding.

### 7.2 Factual Corrections

These include:

- Incorrect dates.
- Incorrect names.
- Wrong publication details.
- Historical inaccuracies.
- Unsupported factual statements.
- Incorrect source descriptions.

Where the error is material, the relevant article should be corrected and the change documented appropriately.

### 7.3 Attribution Corrections

Attribution errors can materially affect the recognition of creators and intellectual contributions.

Examples include:

- Incorrect poet identification.
- Incorrect lyricist attribution.
- Incorrect translation credit.
- Incorrect performer identification.
- Incorrect ownership information.

Substantive attribution corrections should receive particular care.

Where necessary, visible attribution notices should be added.

### 7.4 Translation Corrections

Translation corrections may involve:

- Incorrect vocabulary.
- Distorted meaning.
- Lost ambiguity.
- Misrepresented idioms.
- Incorrect grammatical relationships.
- Unacknowledged adaptation.

Where the correction materially changes the reader's understanding of the literary work, a note explaining the revision may be appropriate.

### 7.5 Scientific Corrections

Scientific corrections may involve errors in:

- Equations.
- Physical mechanisms.
- Constitutive assumptions.
- Mathematical derivations.
- Numerical modelling.
- Experimental interpretation.
- Technical definitions.
- References.

A substantive scientific correction should be based on relevant technical evidence.

Where appropriate, identify whether the original problem concerned a theoretical statement, numerical result, experimental interpretation, or speculative analogy.

### 7.6 Rights and Privacy Corrections

Rights and privacy concerns may require a different response from ordinary content corrections.

Depending on their seriousness, affected material may need to be restricted or removed while the concern is assessed.

Such actions should be proportionate to the issue and applicable obligations.

## 8. Visible Correction Notices

A correction notice may be appropriate when an error materially affects an article's original meaning, attribution, factual accuracy, or scientific interpretation.

The notice should be clear and proportionate.

### 8.1 Recommended Format

**Correction notice**

This article was updated on [date] to correct [brief description of the issue].

The earlier version [brief explanation where useful].

The correction was made following [verification or relevant source information].

### 8.2 Attribution Correction Example

**Correction notice**

The original version of this article incorrectly attributed the literary work to [incorrect attribution].

The attribution has been revised to reflect the verified source information.

### 8.3 Translation Correction Example

**Translation note**

A translation error in the original publication has been corrected. The revised wording better reflects the meaning of the original expression.

### 8.4 Scientific Correction Example

**Scientific correction**

An earlier explanation incorrectly described the reported numerical result as experimental validation.

The wording has been revised to distinguish the computational observation from experimental evidence.

### 8.5 Notice Placement

Where a correction materially affects an article, the notice should be placed where readers can reasonably discover it.

A substantial correction should not be concealed only in an unrelated repository commit message.

## 9. Correction History and Version Control

The publication should preserve an appropriate record of significant editorial changes.

### 9.1 Git History

The repository's Git history provides a technical record of changes to source files.

Use descriptive commit messages for significant corrections.

Examples:

- `Correct literary attribution in article`
- `Revise translation after source verification`
- `Correct scientific explanation of harmonic generation`
- `Update historical reference and citation`

### 9.2 Article Correction Notes

When appropriate, include a concise correction notice within the affected article.

Avoid adding a large correction history to every page for trivial formatting changes.

### 9.3 Changelog

Significant publication-wide corrections or policy changes may also be recorded in:

`CHANGELOG.md`

The changelog should summarise meaningful developments rather than reproduce every minor edit.

### 9.4 Modification Metadata

Where the article's `dateModified` field is used, update it only when a meaningful modification has occurred.

Do not change every article's modification date during routine website builds.

## 10. Corrections to Weekly Collections

A correction to an individual article may affect its corresponding weekly collection.

For example:

- A title correction may affect the displayed article card.
- An author correction may affect weekly metadata.
- A changed permanent URL may affect navigation.
- A withdrawn article may affect the collection's item count.

Where an article is corrected, review its associated weekly page.

The weekly collection should not display information that contradicts the corrected article.

If an article is withdrawn, review the collection rather than leaving a broken article link.

## 11. Search, Sitemap, and Structured Data Updates

Technical corrections may have implications beyond the article itself.

Where relevant, verify:

- Canonical URL.
- Sitemap entry.
- Search index.
- Search result description.
- Open Graph metadata.
- JSON-LD structured data.
- Related article links.
- RSS feed.
- Archive presentation.

For example, an authorship correction may require changes to visible attribution, contributor metadata, and structured descriptions of the original literary work.

Do not leave outdated structured metadata after correcting the visible article.

## 12. Requests Concerning Copyright and Intellectual Property

Copyright concerns should be assessed with particular care.

A report may identify:

- A protected work reproduced without appropriate permission.
- An existing translation reproduced without permission.
- An image with incorrect attribution.
- A recording with unresolved rights.
- An incorrect licence statement.
- A reproduction that exceeds its permitted scope.

### 12.1 Information to Provide

Where possible, identify:

- The affected URL.
- The material concerned.
- The creator or rights holder.
- The original source.
- The relevant rights or licence information.
- The nature of the concern.
- The action requested.

### 12.2 Review and Interim Measures

A credible rights concern may justify temporary restriction of the relevant material while its status is reviewed.

Possible outcomes include:

- Correct attribution.
- Updated licence information.
- Replacement of the material.
- Removal of the affected portion.
- Obtaining appropriate permission.
- Withdrawal of the affected content.

### 12.3 No Assumption of Ownership

A report should not automatically be treated as proof of ownership.

Where ownership or authority is material, appropriate supporting information may be necessary.

The publication should avoid disclosing confidential personal or legal documents unnecessarily.

## 13. Privacy and Sensitive Information

Reports involving private or sensitive information should be treated carefully.

Examples include:

- Personal information published without justification.
- Incorrect identification of a person.
- Confidential correspondence.
- Private photographs or documents.
- Sensitive identifying details.

Where continued public availability creates a serious concern, temporary restriction may be considered while the issue is examined.

The publication should not reproduce sensitive information in a public correction notice.

Refer to `PRIVACY.md`.

## 14. Scientific Disagreements and Research Developments

Scientific knowledge develops through evidence, testing, criticism, and revision.

A newly published study does not automatically invalidate every earlier explanation.

Likewise, a disagreement between researchers does not necessarily establish that one side is factually incorrect.

When a scientific concern is reported, consider:

- Whether the original source remains valid.
- Whether later research changes the evidence.
- Whether the disagreement concerns assumptions or methods.
- Whether the original claim was stated too broadly.
- Whether a model's limitations were adequately explained.
- Whether the article distinguishes theory, simulation, and experiment.

Where evidence remains contested, the article may require qualification rather than a definitive replacement.

The objective is to maintain scientific accuracy without concealing legitimate uncertainty.

## 15. Interpretative Disagreement and Scholarly Debate

Verse & Variable is an interpretative publication.

Literary and philosophical commentary may reflect a particular reading that is not universally accepted.

A reader may propose another interpretation without demonstrating that the existing article contains an error.

Where a disagreement raises an important alternative perspective, the editor may consider:

- Clarifying the existing interpretation.
- Adding an alternative reading.
- Including an additional reference.
- Acknowledging a scholarly disagreement.
- Revising an overly definitive statement.

The publication is not required to replace every interpretation with the view preferred by a reader or contributor.

However, interpretations should not be presented as established historical facts or authorial intentions without appropriate evidence.

## 16. Urgent Concerns

Some reports may require attention beyond ordinary editorial review.

Examples include:

- Unintended disclosure of private information.
- Serious copyright concerns.
- Materially incorrect information that could cause harm if relied upon.
- Security vulnerabilities.
- Exposure of confidential material.
- Incorrectly published sensitive documents.

The affected content may be temporarily restricted while the issue is evaluated.

Such action is precautionary and does not automatically establish that the reported allegation is correct.

Security-related issues should also follow `SECURITY.md`.

## 17. Acknowledgement and Review Expectations

Correction requests will be considered according to their relevance, available evidence, complexity, and potential consequences.

The publication does not guarantee a fixed response time or a particular editorial outcome.

Some concerns may require additional research, consultation of original sources, or examination of conflicting evidence.

A request involving a simple typographical error may be straightforward, while one involving disputed historical authorship or a complex scientific claim may require considerably more investigation.

Submitting a correction request does not automatically guarantee:

- Publication of a response.
- A change to the article.
- Public identification of the person submitting it.
- Removal of the disputed material.
- Acceptance of the proposed wording.

The purpose of the process is to evaluate the concern fairly and responsibly.

## 18. Corrections That Are Not Accepted

A correction request may be declined when:

- The evidence does not support the proposed change.
- The matter concerns a legitimate difference of interpretation.
- The existing statement is adequately supported.
- The requested modification would introduce a new inaccuracy.
- The material is clearly presented as an opinion or hypothesis.
- The report does not identify a sufficiently specific issue.
- The requested change conflicts with the verified original source.

Where appropriate, the existing article may still be clarified to make its limitations or interpretative status more explicit.

A request being declined does not mean that the subject is permanently closed to further evidence.

New and relevant supporting information may justify reconsideration.

## 19. Repeated or Duplicate Reports

Multiple reports may concern the same issue.

Where several people identify the same underlying problem, the publication may consolidate the review into one correction process.

Repeated submissions do not necessarily require separate public correction notices.

The number of reports should not replace evaluation of the actual evidence.

## 20. Record Keeping and Confidentiality

Correction records should be maintained in a proportionate and responsible manner.

Relevant internal records may include:

- Article URL.
- Date of report.
- Nature of the concern.
- Supporting sources.
- Review notes.
- Editorial decision.
- Correction made.
- Relevant publication history.

Records should contain only information reasonably necessary for the purpose.

Private correspondence, sensitive personal details, and confidential supporting documents should not be published merely because they were submitted as correction evidence.

Public correction notices should explain the relevant editorial change without unnecessarily identifying the person who reported it.

## 23. Continuing Commitment

An independent literary and intellectual publication must remain willing to reconsider its own work.

Accuracy is not demonstrated by claiming that errors never occur. It is demonstrated through careful research, transparent attribution, responsible correction, and a willingness to improve when reliable evidence warrants it.

Verse & Variable seeks to maintain an archive in which literary interpretation remains open, scientific explanations remain accountable to evidence, original creators receive proper recognition, and published material can be corrected without compromising editorial independence.
