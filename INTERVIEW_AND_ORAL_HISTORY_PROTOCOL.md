## 1. Purpose

This protocol establishes a practical procedure for conducting interviews and incorporating oral-history material into Verse & Variable.

Interviews and oral histories can preserve experiences, literary traditions, memories, artistic practices, historical accounts, and specialist knowledge that may not be adequately represented in published sources.

They also involve responsibilities toward the individuals who contribute their time, experiences, voices, memories, and knowledge.

This document explains how the publication should:

- Plan interviews responsibly.
- Identify and approach potential participants.
- Explain the purpose of participation.
- Obtain informed and voluntary consent.
- Record and document contributions appropriately.
- Prepare accurate transcripts.
- Translate multilingual contributions responsibly.
- Verify quotations and contextual information.
- Protect personal and sensitive information.
- Address withdrawal and correction requests.
- Prepare interview material for publication.

The protocol applies to interviews, recorded conversations, oral-history contributions, and similar forms of first-person testimony.

It does not establish a formal institutional research protocol or replace any required ethics approval, professional authorisation, or legal compliance.

## 2. Guiding Principles

Interview-based material should be handled according to the following principles.

### 2.1 Voluntary Participation

Participation should be voluntary. Individuals should understand what they are being asked to contribute and should not be pressured into participating.

### 2.2 Informed Consent

Participants should receive sufficient information to make a meaningful decision about participation, recording, identification, quotation, and publication.

### 2.3 Respect

Interviews should be conducted with patience, cultural sensitivity, and respect for the participant's dignity and personal boundaries.

### 2.4 Accuracy

Statements should be documented and represented faithfully.

The publication should not invent, distort, or selectively reconstruct a participant's account.

### 2.5 Privacy

Personal information should be collected and disclosed only to the extent reasonably necessary for the agreed editorial purpose.

### 2.6 Transparency

Participants should understand how their contribution may be used, edited, translated, attributed, stored, and published.

### 2.7 Fairness

A participant's words should be represented in context, without misleading editing or selective presentation.

### 2.8 Editorial Independence

Participation does not automatically give an interviewee control over the entire article or its editorial conclusions.

At the same time, editorial independence does not justify misrepresenting the participant's statements.

## 3. Types of Interview and Oral-History Material

The appropriate procedure depends on the nature of the contribution.

### 3.1 Personal Interviews

Conversations with individuals about their experiences, memories, knowledge, professional work, creative practice, or perspectives.

### 3.2 Literary Interviews

Conversations with poets, writers, translators, lyricists, editors, performers, literary scholars, and other contributors to literary traditions.

### 3.3 Specialist Interviews

Conversations with researchers, engineers, scientists, historians, linguists, philosophers, and other subject specialists.

### 3.4 Oral Histories

Recorded or documented recollections intended to preserve individual or community experiences, historical memory, and cultural knowledge.

### 3.5 Community Contributions

Contributions from individuals sharing knowledge about linguistic, regional, artistic, cultural, or traditional practices.

A single participant should not automatically be represented as speaking for an entire community.

### 3.6 Documentary Conversations

Conversations intended to clarify the background of a document, research project, historical record, creative work, or other source.

These categories may overlap. The editor should identify the principal purpose before requesting participation.

## 4. Before Approaching a Participant

The interview should have a clear editorial purpose.

### 4.1 Define the Subject

Identify:

- The proposed interview subject.
- The article or project in which the contribution may appear.
- The principal questions to be explored.
- The intended audience.
- Whether the conversation is informational, historical, literary, scientific, or personal.

### 4.2 Establish Relevance

An interview should contribute meaningful information, context, experience, or perspective.

Do not conduct an interview merely to obtain a quotation that supports a predetermined conclusion.

### 4.3 Determine the Appropriate Participant

Consider whether the individual has relevant knowledge or direct experience.

Where a topic requires specialist expertise, distinguish between a participant's personal account and an expert assessment.

### 4.4 Identify Potential Risks

Consider whether the interview may involve:

- Sensitive personal experiences.
- Health or medical information.
- Family or professional disputes.
- Institutional or workplace confidentiality.
- Political or social vulnerability.
- Potential reputational harm.
- Children or vulnerable individuals.
- Private cultural or community knowledge.
- Legal or safety concerns.

Where significant risks exist, obtain appropriate specialist guidance and any required approval before proceeding.

### 4.5 Check Required Permissions

Determine whether the interview requires additional permissions, institutional review, or legal authorisation.

Do not assume that an independent publication is exempt from requirements that otherwise apply to the proposed activity.

## 5. Inviting a Participant

The initial invitation should clearly explain the nature of the proposed conversation.

Where possible, provide the following information before scheduling:

- The name of the publication.
- A short description of Verse & Variable.
- The purpose of the interview.
- The intended topic.
- The approximate interview format.
- Whether recording is proposed.
- Whether the contribution may be published.
- Whether the participant may be identified.
- Whether translation or editing may be involved.
- Whether participation is voluntary.

Avoid exaggerated claims about the reach, institutional status, or significance of the publication.

### 5.1 No Implied Obligation

Do not imply that participation is required for professional, academic, personal, or institutional reasons.

### 5.2 No Misleading Promises

Do not promise:

- Guaranteed publication.
- Guaranteed anonymity without assessing identification risks.
- Complete editorial control.
- Indefinite confidentiality where it cannot be guaranteed.
- Absolute deletion of all copies after withdrawal.
- Publication in a particular format that has not been confirmed.

### 5.3 Declining an Invitation

An individual should be able to decline without being asked to justify the decision.

## 6. Informed Consent

Informed consent should be obtained before an interview begins or before any recording or other activity for which consent is required.

The explanation should be understandable to the participant.

### 6.1 Information to Provide

Explain:

1. Who is conducting the interview.
2. The purpose of the interview.
3. The intended publication and audience.
4. Whether audio, video, photography, or written notes will be used.
5. How the material may be edited and translated.
6. Whether quotations will be published.
7. How the participant will be identified.
8. Whether the interview may be archived.
9. How personal information will be handled.
10. Whether the participant may decline questions.
11. How withdrawal and correction requests may be handled.
12. Any important limitations on confidentiality or withdrawal.

### 6.2 Separate Permissions

Where appropriate, seek separate consent for:

- Participation.
- Audio recording.
- Video recording.
- Photography.
- Use of the participant's name.
- Publication of direct quotations.
- Publication of photographs or voice recordings.
- Translation.
- Long-term archival retention.
- Reuse in future publication formats.

Consent to one activity should not automatically be treated as consent to every other activity.

### 6.3 Recording Consent

Recording should not begin until the participant has agreed to the proposed recording method.

A participant who agrees to an interview does not necessarily agree to being recorded.

### 6.4 Ongoing Consent

Consent is not merely a form completed at the beginning.

If the interview changes substantially in purpose or involves new forms of disclosure, explain the change and seek additional agreement where appropriate.

### 6.5 Documentation

Record how and when consent was obtained.

The record may be written, electronic, or otherwise appropriately documented.

Where written consent is not practical, document the alternative procedure and the participant's agreement.

## 8. Planning the Interview

Prepare a proportionate interview plan.

### 8.1 Questions

Questions should be:

- Clear and understandable.
- Relevant to the stated purpose.
- Appropriate to the participant's background.
- Open-ended where detailed responses are useful.
- Non-leading wherever practicable.
- Sensitive to personal and cultural context.

### 8.2 Avoid Presuppositions

Do not frame a question in a way that assumes a disputed statement is already true.

For example, avoid:

"Why did this event happen in the way described?"

when the underlying event or description has not yet been established.

Prefer:

"How do you remember this event, and what information helps explain it?"

### 8.3 Avoid Unnecessary Intrusion

Do not ask for intimate or sensitive details unless they are genuinely necessary for the declared purpose and appropriate safeguards are in place.

### 8.4 Prepare Follow-up Questions

Allow participants to clarify, qualify, or correct their initial answers.

Do not interrupt simply to force a preferred narrative.

### 8.5 Language

Whenever practical, conduct the interview in a language in which the participant can communicate comfortably.

Do not assume that a participant's fluency in one language implies equal comfort with another.

## 9. Conducting the Interview

### 9.1 Opening

At the beginning:

- Introduce the interviewer.
- Restate the purpose.
- Confirm the participant's understanding.
- Confirm applicable consent.
- Confirm recording arrangements.
- Explain that questions may be declined.
- Invite clarification before proceeding.

### 9.2 Respectful Communication

Allow sufficient time for the participant to respond.

Avoid:

- Intimidation.
- Repeated pressure.
- Manipulative questioning.
- Unnecessary confrontation.
- Deliberate misrepresentation.
- Promises that cannot be honoured.

### 9.3 Difficult Topics

Where the discussion becomes distressing or unexpectedly sensitive:

- Pause the interview.
- Ask whether the participant wishes to continue.
- Do not pressure the participant to provide further details.
- Avoid treating emotional distress as evidence of accuracy.
- Consider whether the subject should be omitted or handled with additional safeguards.

The interviewer should not attempt to provide professional counselling or specialist intervention unless appropriately qualified and engaged in that role.

### 9.4 Participant Corrections

Allow the participant to clarify statements during the interview.

Where a statement is ambiguous, ask a neutral follow-up question rather than assuming its intended meaning.

### 9.5 Closing

At the end:

- Confirm any outstanding clarification.
- Explain the anticipated editorial process.
- Restate any agreed identification or confidentiality conditions.
- Explain how follow-up communication may occur.
- Thank the participant.

Do not imply that the interview guarantees publication.

## 10. Audio, Video, and Photographic Recording

Recordings may preserve useful information, but they also contain personal and contextual information that requires protection.

### 10.1 Recording Quality

Use a recording method appropriate to the purpose.

Avoid unnecessary recording of unrelated conversations or third parties.

### 10.2 Third Parties

Where other identifiable people appear or speak in a recording, consider their privacy and any required permission before publication.

### 10.3 Recording Identification

Use a clear internal identifier.

Example:

`VV-INT-2026-001`

This should not contain unnecessary personal identifiers.

### 10.4 Original Recording

Retain an unaltered original where a recording is needed for accurate transcription or verification.

Any edited version should be distinguishable from the original.

### 10.5 Edited Excerpts

Do not edit a recording to change the meaning of the participant's statement.

Where an excerpt is shortened, preserve the context necessary for fair interpretation.

### 10.6 Recording Security

Store recordings in an appropriately protected location.

Do not publish private recording links or leave sensitive audio or video files publicly accessible.

## 11. Transcription

A transcript should accurately represent the recorded or documented conversation.

### 11.1 Verbatim and Edited Transcripts

Distinguish between:

**Verbatim transcript:** A close representation of the spoken words, including relevant repetitions, pauses, and expressions where appropriate.

**Lightly edited transcript:** A transcript with limited adjustments for readability that preserve the original meaning.

**Edited interview:** A substantially shortened or reorganised presentation, with editing disclosed where necessary.

Do not present a substantially rewritten interview as an unedited verbatim transcript.

### 11.2 Inaudible or Unclear Speech

Mark unclear passages rather than inventing missing words.

Possible notation:

- `[inaudible]`
- `[unclear]`
- `[overlapping speech]`
- `[recording interruption]`

### 11.3 Non-Verbal Information

Include non-verbal observations only when relevant to the purpose and supported by the recording or contemporaneous notes.

Do not infer emotions, intentions, or reactions without an adequate basis.

### 11.4 Speaker Identification

Identify speakers accurately.

Avoid assigning statements to the wrong individual when conversations involve multiple participants.

### 11.5 Review Against the Recording

Where possible, compare the transcript with the original recording before publication.

Machine-generated transcription should be checked for accuracy.

## 12. Translation of Interview Material

Translation should preserve the participant's meaning and identify the role of the translator.

### 12.1 Original Language

Retain the original-language record where it is appropriate and lawful to do so.

### 12.2 Translation Accuracy

Check:

- Meaning.
- Grammar.
- Idiomatic expression.
- Cultural references.
- Regional terminology.
- Technical terms.
- Ambiguity.
- Tone.

### 12.3 Translation Disclosure

Identify whether the published text is:

- The original-language transcript.
- A human-prepared translation.
- A collaboratively prepared translation.
- A machine-assisted translation.
- An edited or adapted rendering.

### 12.4 Ambiguous Expressions

Where a statement permits more than one interpretation, do not silently choose the interpretation most convenient to the article.

Record relevant ambiguity where necessary.

### 12.5 Cultural and Regional Expressions

Preserve expressions that carry important cultural or regional meaning.

Explain them where a direct equivalent does not exist.

### 12.6 Translator Attribution

Identify the translator where appropriate and obtain permission where required.

Do not attribute the interviewee's translated words to the interviewee as though they personally prepared the translation.

## 13. Quotation Verification

Direct quotations should accurately reflect the participant's statements.

### 13.1 Context

Check that a quotation has not been separated from context in a way that changes its meaning.

### 13.2 Accuracy

Check:

- The wording.
- The speaker.
- The sequence of statements.
- Relevant qualifications.
- The language of the original statement.
- Any editorial omissions.

### 13.3 Ellipses and Omissions

Use ellipses or other omission markers responsibly.

Do not remove qualifying language merely to make a quotation more persuasive.

### 13.4 Translated Quotations

Where quotations are translated, the published wording should not be represented as an exact quotation in the original language.

Identify the translation appropriately.

### 13.5 Paraphrasing

Paraphrased material should not be placed within quotation marks.

Clearly distinguish the editor's summary from the participant's words.

## 14. Participant Review and Factual Clarification

Where appropriate, the participant may be invited to review selected quotations or passages concerning their contribution.

### 14.1 Purpose

Participant review can help identify:

- Transcription errors.
- Incorrect names or dates.
- Misunderstood terminology.
- Translation errors.
- Missing context.
- Accidental disclosure of sensitive information.

### 14.2 Editorial Independence

Reviewing quotations does not automatically give the participant authority over the entire article, its independent research, or its editorial conclusions.

### 14.3 Disputed Interpretations

Where the participant disagrees with the editor's interpretation, assess the disagreement fairly.

Distinguish between:

- An inaccurate quotation.
- A factual error.
- A translation problem.
- A missing qualification.
- An editorial interpretation.
- A disagreement about the implications of an account.

### 14.4 Record of Changes

Where substantial changes are made following participant feedback, maintain an appropriate editorial record.

### 14.5 No Automatic Approval Claim

Do not state that a participant approved the entire article merely because they checked selected quotations.

## 15. Historical Memory and Oral-History Interpretation

Oral histories are valuable sources of personal experience and historical memory.

They require careful interpretation.

### 15.1 Memory and Historical Fact

A person's memory is evidence of their recollection.

It is not automatically independent confirmation of every date, event, statement, or interpretation contained in that recollection.

### 15.2 Chronology

Where important, compare recollections with independent documentary or historical sources.

### 15.3 Contradictory Accounts

Different participants may remember the same event differently.

Preserve meaningful differences rather than forcing artificial agreement.

### 15.4 Retrospective Interpretation

Participants may interpret earlier events in light of later experiences.

Distinguish their present understanding from what they documented or believed at the time.

### 15.5 Historical Limitations

Where corroboration is unavailable, make that limitation clear.

Do not invent documentary confirmation to strengthen an oral-history account.

### 15.6 Community Memory

A contribution may represent an individual perspective within a larger tradition.

Avoid presenting one recollection as the complete history of a community.

## 16. Anonymity and Pseudonymity

Some participants may wish to contribute without public identification.

### 16.1 Agreed Identity

Record the participant's identity securely where necessary for editorial administration, while keeping it separate from the public-facing article.

### 16.2 Pseudonyms

A pseudonym should be used consistently and should not create a misleading impression of a different real person.

### 16.3 Indirect Identification

Even without a name, an individual may be identifiable through their occupation, location, experiences, relatives, or distinctive statements.

Assess the combined disclosure risk.

### 16.4 Limits of Anonymity

Explain that anonymity cannot always be guaranteed, particularly where:

- The material is already publicly known.
- The participant's identity is apparent from context.
- Other people possess the same information.
- Legal obligations require disclosure.
- Third-party systems retain copies.

Do not offer stronger confidentiality guarantees than can reasonably be maintained.

## 17. Sensitive and Vulnerable Participants

Additional safeguards are necessary when interviews concern individuals who may face increased risks.

### 17.1 Children

Do not conduct interviews involving children for publication without appropriate safeguards, applicable permissions, and any required ethical or institutional approval.

Age-appropriate assent and parental or guardian permission may be necessary, depending on the circumstances.

### 17.2 Vulnerable Adults

Consider whether a participant may be affected by illness, dependency, coercion, limited decision-making capacity, or other circumstances that affect voluntary participation.

### 17.3 Personal Trauma

Do not encourage unnecessary disclosure of traumatic experiences.

Avoid sensationalising personal suffering.

### 17.4 Power Imbalances

Where the interviewer has authority or influence over the participant, take additional steps to ensure that participation is voluntary.

### 17.5 Safety

Do not publish details that foreseeably expose participants or other individuals to retaliation, harassment, discrimination, or physical danger without careful ethical assessment.

Where the risks cannot be appropriately managed, do not publish the affected material.

## 18. Data Protection, Storage, and Retention

Interview records may include names, voices, contact details, personal experiences, and confidential information.

They should be handled according to their sensitivity.

### 18.1 Record Types

Separate records where practical:

- Consent documents.
- Contact information.
- Original recordings.
- Transcripts.
- Translations.
- Editorial notes.
- Published quotations.
- Permission records.

### 18.2 Access

Only individuals with a legitimate editorial need should have access to sensitive interview material.

### 18.3 File Naming

Use neutral identifiers rather than placing sensitive personal information in filenames.

Example:

`VV-INT-2026-001-audio-original`

### 18.4 Retention Period

Retain material only for as long as a legitimate editorial, verification, accountability, or legal purpose requires.

The appropriate period depends on the nature of the contribution and any applicable obligations.

### 18.5 Deletion

Where material is no longer required, consider secure deletion.

Take account of any copies held by external recording, transcription, cloud-storage, or communication services.

### 18.6 Third-Party Services

Before uploading recordings or transcripts to transcription platforms, cloud tools, or AI systems, consider:

- Privacy terms.
- Data-processing arrangements.
- Retention practices.
- Access controls.
- Whether the service may use submitted material for training.
- Whether the participant's consent covers the proposed processing.

Do not upload confidential or sensitive material without an appropriate basis and safeguards.

## 19. Withdrawal Requests

Participants may raise concerns or request withdrawal of their contributions.

The practical response depends on the stage of the publication process and the applicable permissions and obligations.

### 19.1 Before Publication

Where a participant withdraws consent before publication, suspend the proposed use of the contribution while assessing the request and any relevant conditions.

Do not assume that an unpublished contribution can automatically be used merely because it has already been recorded.

### 19.2 After Publication

Where a request is received after publication, consider:

- The original consent arrangement.
- The nature of the concern.
- The sensitivity of the disclosed information.
- The possibility of harm.
- Whether the material can be corrected, anonymised, restricted, or removed.
- Relevant legal and editorial obligations.

### 19.3 Third-Party Copies

Explain that copies may remain in backups, archives, search engines, quotations, or third-party systems even after an article is amended or removed from the publication website.

### 19.4 No Retaliation

A participant should not be treated adversely merely for raising a good-faith concern or requesting a review of their contribution.

### 19.5 Escalation

Significant privacy, safety, confidentiality, or legal concerns should be assessed promptly and referred for appropriate specialist advice where necessary.

## 20. Publishing an Interview or Oral-History Contribution

Before publication, the editor should confirm that the contribution has been appropriately prepared.

### 20.1 Article Context

Explain why the interview is relevant to the article.

Avoid using a personal contribution simply as decoration or an authoritative-sounding endorsement.

### 20.2 Attribution

Identify the participant in accordance with the agreed attribution arrangement.

### 20.3 Interview Date

Provide the interview date where useful and appropriate.

### 20.4 Language

Identify the original language and any relevant translation process when it materially affects interpretation.

### 20.5 Recording and Transcript Status

Distinguish between:

- A direct interview transcript.
- Selected interview excerpts.
- An edited interview.
- An oral-history summary.
- A paraphrased recollection.

### 20.6 Corrections and Context

Resolve material transcription, translation, attribution, and privacy issues before publication.

### 20.7 Disclosures

Disclose relevant relationships, funding, editorial assistance, and conflicts of interest where necessary.

### 20.8 No Misleading Endorsement

Do not use the participant's name, professional title, institutional affiliation, or photograph in a way that implies endorsement of unrelated opinions or the publication as a whole.

## 24. Continuing Commitment

Every interview is an act of trust.

A person who shares a memory, a literary interpretation, a professional experience, or a part of their personal history is contributing more than information. They are allowing an aspect of their knowledge or experience to enter a public intellectual record.

That contribution deserves accuracy, context, respect, and care.

Verse & Variable will approach interviews and oral histories with the understanding that preserving a person's words also means respecting the circumstances in which those words were shared.

**Listen carefully. Record honestly. Translate responsibly. Publish with respect.**
