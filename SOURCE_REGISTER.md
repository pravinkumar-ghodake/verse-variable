## 1. Purpose

The Source Register establishes a consistent method for recording the sources used in preparing individual literary explorations published on Verse & Variable.

The publication may draw upon original literary works, translations, historical records, linguistic references, philosophical texts, scientific publications, archival material, and other research resources.

A structured source record helps ensure that:

- Original works can be identified and traced.
- Literary attribution is supported by reliable information.
- Translations and adaptations are distinguished from original texts.
- Historical and cultural statements have identifiable references.
- Scientific explanations are supported by appropriate research.
- Copyright and licensing conditions are considered.
- Future corrections can be made using the original research trail.

The register is intended to complement the published article's bibliography and citations.

## 2. Scope

A source register may contain references from the following categories:

1. Original literary works.
2. Literary editions and collections.
3. Original recordings and performances.
4. Translations and transliterations.
5. Historical and biographical sources.
6. Linguistic and dictionary resources.
7. Philosophical and cultural scholarship.
8. Scientific research publications.
9. Technical documentation and standards.
10. Archival and institutional resources.
11. Images, illustrations, and multimedia.
12. Other relevant supporting material.

Not every article requires every category.

Only sources that are relevant to the particular exploration should be included.

## 3. Source Identification

Every source should be recorded using information appropriate to its type.

Where available, record:

- Source identification number.
- Author or creator.
- Title.
- Publication or source name.
- Publisher or institution.
- Publication date.
- Edition or version.
- Volume, issue, and page numbers.
- DOI, ISBN, ISSN, or other persistent identifier.
- Original source URL.
- Access date, where relevant.
- Language.
- Source category.
- Research purpose.
- Verification status.

Do not invent missing bibliographic details.

Where information cannot be established, mark it as unknown or unavailable rather than presenting an assumption as fact.

## 4. Source Categories

### 4.1 Original Literary Sources

These sources establish the original literary work being explored.

Examples:

- Authoritative poetry collections.
- Published books.
- Original manuscripts.
- Literary archives.
- Original song recordings.
- Authorised lyrics or published texts.
- Reliable primary documentary sources.

Record the edition, version, recording, or archival reference whenever possible.

For literary works with variant readings, identify which version is being used.

### 4.2 Translation Sources

These sources provide translated versions of the original work.

Record:

- Translator.
- Original author.
- Translated title, where applicable.
- Source publication.
- Publication date.
- Edition.
- Page or section.
- Reproduction rights or licence information.

Distinguish an independently prepared translation from a published translation.

A translation should never be attributed to the original poet or author unless that person actually prepared it.

### 4.3 Linguistic Sources

These sources help establish the meaning, usage, pronunciation, or historical development of words and expressions.

Examples:

- Authoritative dictionaries.
- Etymological references.
- Historical language dictionaries.
- Academic linguistic studies.
- Grammar references.
- Regional and dialectal resources.
- Script and transliteration standards.

Where an interpretation depends on a particular word, record the relevant entry, page, or section.

### 4.4 Historical and Cultural Sources

These sources support contextual claims concerning:

- Historical periods.
- Literary movements.
- Cultural practices.
- Geographic context.
- Biographical information.
- Social and political circumstances.
- Philosophical traditions.
- Historical events.

Prefer reliable archival, scholarly, and institutional sources where available.

Distinguish historical evidence from later interpretation.

### 4.5 Philosophical Sources

Record the original philosophical text or primary reference whenever possible.

Secondary scholarship may be used to explain:

- Conceptual frameworks.
- Intellectual traditions.
- Historical development.
- Different interpretations.
- Comparative philosophical perspectives.

Do not attribute a philosophical position to a thinker solely because a secondary source associates similar terminology with that person.

### 4.6 Scientific Sources

Scientific references should identify the research supporting technical explanations.

Relevant sources may include:

- Original research papers.
- Review articles.
- Academic textbooks.
- Research monographs.
- Conference proceedings.
- Validated experimental studies.
- Technical standards.
- Official software documentation.
- Reproducible datasets.

Record publication status where relevant, including whether a work is peer-reviewed, a preprint, a technical report, or another type of research output.

### 4.7 Technical and Computational Sources

For computational methods, numerical examples, software, and engineering concepts, document relevant technical sources.

Possible information includes:

- Software or library name.
- Version.
- Developer or organisation.
- Official documentation.
- Mathematical formulation.
- Applicable standard.
- Licence.
- Relevant model or method.
- Date of access.

Software documentation should not be treated as independent experimental validation of a scientific claim.

### 4.8 Visual and Multimedia Sources

For images, figures, photographs, recordings, and videos, record:

- Asset title or description.
- Original creator.
- Source location.
- Rights holder, where identified.
- Licence or permission.
- Attribution conditions.
- Modifications.
- Article or page where used.

A citation alone does not establish permission to reproduce protected material.

Refer to `ASSET_CREDITS.md` for the publication's asset attribution requirements.

## 5. Verification Status

Every source should have a clear verification status.

Use the following classifications:

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Verified | Source identity and relevant information have been checked |
| Partially verified | Some details have been confirmed, but other details remain uncertain |
| Unverified | Source has been identified but not adequately checked |
| Disputed | Reliable sources provide conflicting information |
| Inaccessible | Original source could not currently be accessed |
| Superseded | A newer or corrected version has replaced the referenced material |
| Rejected | Source was assessed and found unsuitable for the intended claim |

Verification should refer to the specific information being used, not simply the existence of the source.

For example, the existence of a book may be verified while a particular attribution taken from that book remains disputed.

## 6. Research Relevance

A source should be connected to the claim, interpretation, or material it supports.

The register should explain why the source was consulted.

Examples:

- Verification of original poetic wording.
- Confirmation of authorship.
- Identification of a lyricist or composer.
- Interpretation of a regional expression.
- Historical context of a literary movement.
- Translation comparison.
- Explanation of a scientific principle.
- Verification of an experimental result.
- Support for a mathematical formulation.
- Copyright and reproduction assessment.

A source should not be included merely to make the bibliography appear extensive.

## 8. Referencing in Published Articles

The source register supports the research process, while the published article should provide references that readers can reasonably follow.

Depending on the nature of the article, this may include:

- Source notes.
- Bibliographic references.
- DOI links.
- Publisher or archive links.
- Original recording references.
- Translation credits.
- Relevant scientific citations.
- Image and asset credits.

References should be placed close enough to the relevant claims that readers can understand what each source supports.

A general bibliography should not substitute for precise attribution of a quotation, translation, illustration, or technical claim.

## 9. Source Conflicts and Disputed Information

When multiple sources disagree, record the disagreement rather than silently selecting the most convenient account.

The register should preserve:

- The competing claims.
- The sources supporting each claim.
- Their publication dates.
- Their authority and proximity to the original material.
- The reason for the editorial conclusion.
- Any unresolved uncertainty.

Where the issue materially affects the article, the published text should communicate the uncertainty.

An unresolved question should not be converted into a definitive statement merely because the article requires a conclusion.

## 10. Source Updates and Corrections

Sources may become outdated, corrected, retracted, inaccessible, or superseded.

When a relevant source changes:

1. Revisit the claim it supports.
2. Confirm the source's updated status.
3. Identify whether the article requires revision.
4. Replace or supplement the reference where appropriate.
5. Record significant changes in the article's research notes.
6. Update the public article and changelog where justified.

Retracted or corrected scientific publications should not be cited as though their original conclusions remain unqualified.

## 11. Research Integrity and Responsible Record Keeping

The source register should remain accurate and useful.

Do not:

- Invent references.
- Record unconsulted works as though they were verified.
- Use fabricated DOIs or publication details.
- Present search-engine snippets as original sources.
- Treat an AI-generated citation as verified.
- Attribute a translation without checking its origin.
- Hide conflicting information.
- Claim experimental validation from a numerical model.
- Treat public accessibility as permission to reproduce copyrighted material.

Internal research records should not contain confidential manuscripts, private correspondence, sensitive personal information, or restricted material unless there is a legitimate and appropriately protected reason to retain it.

Only information suitable for public disclosure should be placed in a publicly published register.

## 13. Maintenance

The source register format may be updated as the publication's research practices develop.

Additional fields may be introduced for particular literary forms, languages, scientific disciplines, or types of source material.

The objective is not to create unnecessary bureaucracy. It is to preserve a reliable connection between published statements and the sources that support them.
