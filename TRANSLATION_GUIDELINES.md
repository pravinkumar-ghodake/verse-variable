## 1. Purpose

This document establishes the principles and practices governing translations published on Verse & Variable.

The publication brings together literary expressions from Urdu, Hindi, Marathi, Kannada, Tamil, Malayalam, Telugu, other Indian and South Asian languages, and relevant international literary traditions.

Translation makes these expressions accessible across linguistic boundaries. However, it also introduces interpretative decisions that can affect meaning, tone, rhythm, cultural references, and literary identity.

These guidelines aim to ensure that translations remain faithful to the spirit and substance of the original while acknowledging the inevitable differences between languages.

They apply to:

- Poetry and verse.
- Ghazals, nazms, rubaiyat, and shayari.
- Song lyrics.
- Folk expressions.
- Literary prose.
- Philosophical writing.
- Dramatic and spoken-word material.
- Selected historical and cultural texts.

## 2. Foundational Principles

### 2.1 The Original Remains Primary

The original literary expression is the primary work.

A translation is a representation of that work in another language. It should never silently replace the original or be presented as an equivalent in every literary, linguistic, or cultural respect.

Where reasonably possible, the original script should accompany its translation.

### 2.2 Meaning Before Mechanical Word Substitution

A translation should communicate the intended meaning of the original expression rather than mechanically replacing each word with a dictionary equivalent.

Literal accuracy is important, but word-for-word substitution can produce misleading, unnatural, or culturally inaccurate results.

The translator should consider the meaning of an expression within its complete linguistic and literary context.

### 2.3 Respect for Literary Identity

The translation should preserve the distinctive character of the original work as far as reasonably possible.

This may involve attention to:

- Imagery.
- Metaphor.
- Symbolism.
- Emotional intensity.
- Voice and perspective.
- Rhythm and cadence.
- Repetition.
- Wordplay.
- Ambiguity.
- Literary structure.
- Cultural associations.

These elements should not be sacrificed casually for superficial fluency.

### 2.4 Transparency About Interpretative Choices

A translation necessarily involves decisions.

Where an important expression permits multiple meanings, the translation should not conceal that ambiguity merely to produce a more convenient English sentence.

Relevant alternatives may be explained through a translator's note or brief editorial commentary.

### 2.5 No Claim of Perfect Equivalence

No translation should be described as a perfect or definitive reproduction of the original.

Languages differ in grammar, sound systems, idiom, cultural references, poetic conventions, and expressive possibilities.

A successful translation aims for meaningful communication while remaining honest about what cannot be reproduced fully.

## 3. Identifying the Type of Translation

Every translation should be understood according to how it was prepared.

### 3.1 Published Translation

A published translation is an existing translation prepared by an identified translator and made available through a book, journal, authorised publication, archive, or other source.

When using an existing translation:

- Identify the translator.
- Identify the publication or original source.
- Record relevant edition and publication information.
- Preserve the translated wording accurately.
- Review the applicable copyright and reproduction conditions.
- Do not silently modify the text.
- Identify omissions or adaptations where applicable.

A translation should not be attributed to the original poet unless the poet actually prepared it.

### 3.2 Original Translation

An original translation is prepared specifically for Verse & Variable from the original literary work.

Where this is the case, identify it transparently.

Suggested wording:

**English translation:** Original translation prepared for Verse & Variable.

The translation should be checked against the original script and reliable linguistic sources wherever necessary.

### 3.3 Adapted Translation

An adapted translation involves deliberate changes to the original wording, structure, expression, or presentation.

Adaptations may be appropriate for certain literary or explanatory purposes, but they must not be presented as faithful reproductions of an unchanged original.

Identify substantial adaptations clearly.

### 3.4 Interpretative Translation

An interpretative translation seeks to communicate a particular reading of a literary expression, including its emotional, philosophical, or cultural implications.

Such a translation may depart from literal wording to preserve an important dimension of the original.

Interpretative choices should be defensible and should not introduce meanings unsupported by the source.

### 3.5 Literal or Close Translation

A close translation prioritises the source wording and grammatical structure.

It may help readers examine how the original expression is constructed.

However, grammatical closeness does not automatically guarantee literary accuracy.

Where a close translation becomes unnatural or confusing in English, supplementary explanation may be more useful than forced word substitution.

## 4. Working from the Original Language

Translation should begin with the original expression wherever it is available.

### 4.1 Original-Script Verification

Before translating:

- Identify the original language and script.
- Verify the text against a reliable source.
- Check the relevant edition or version.
- Identify spelling and textual variants.
- Preserve the original line or stanza order where appropriate.
- Establish whether the work is written, performed, adapted, or transmitted orally.

Do not translate from an unattributed quotation or an unverified English rendering when the original text is available for consultation.

### 4.2 Linguistic Context

Words should be interpreted in the context in which they appear.

Consider:

- Grammatical structure.
- Idiomatic usage.
- Regional vocabulary.
- Historical meaning.
- Register and formality.
- Figurative usage.
- Cultural associations.
- Multiple possible interpretations.

A dictionary definition can help establish meaning, but it should not replace contextual interpretation.

### 4.3 Dialect and Regional Expression

Where the original uses regional speech, dialect, or locally meaningful vocabulary, preserve its character as far as practical.

Do not automatically replace regional expressions with standardised equivalents if doing so removes an important part of the work's identity.

Where necessary, provide a short explanation rather than flattening the expression into generic English.

## 5. Preserving Literary Meaning

### 5.1 Literal Meaning and Implied Meaning

Many literary expressions communicate more than their direct grammatical meaning.

A translation should consider both:

- What the words explicitly say.
- What the expression reasonably suggests within its literary context.

However, an implied meaning should not be inserted as though it were explicitly stated by the original author.

When the difference matters, a translator's note may explain the interpretative choice.

### 5.2 Metaphor and Symbolism

Metaphors, symbols, and figurative expressions should be preserved whenever an intelligible equivalent exists.

Do not replace an original image with an unrelated image simply because the alternative sounds more familiar in English.

Where a metaphor cannot be transferred naturally, consider preserving the original image with a short explanatory note.

### 5.3 Ambiguity

Ambiguity can be an intentional literary feature.

A word may carry emotional, philosophical, romantic, spiritual, or culturally specific meanings simultaneously.

The translation should preserve multiple possibilities where English can reasonably accommodate them.

If English requires a more specific choice, explain the relevant limitation where it materially affects interpretation.

### 5.4 Emotional Register

A translation should pay attention to the emotional character of the original.

For example:

- Restraint should not become melodrama.
- Irony should not become literal sincerity.
- Simplicity should not become artificial sophistication.
- Intimacy should not become exaggerated sentimentality.
- Philosophical subtlety should not become an unsupported moral lesson.

The language should remain proportionate to the original expression.

## 6. Poetry, Rhythm, and Literary Form

Poetry presents particular translation challenges because meaning is carried not only through words but also through sound, rhythm, repetition, structure, and form.

### 6.1 Preserve the Form Where Meaningful

Where appropriate, identify and retain the structure of the original work.

This may include:

- Couplet organisation.
- Stanza structure.
- Refrain.
- Repeated phrases.
- Rhyme scheme.
- Meter.
- Line breaks.
- Parallel constructions.
- Formal poetic conventions.

A translation does not need to reproduce every formal feature if doing so would seriously distort meaning.

Any substantial departure should be understood as a translation decision rather than an invisible property of the original.

### 6.2 Rhythm and Cadence

Pay attention to the movement and rhythm of the source.

A translation should aim to retain a natural literary cadence where possible.

However, forcing an English meter or rhyme scheme at the expense of the original meaning is discouraged.

### 6.3 Rhyme

Rhyme may be an important element of a poem, but it should not become the sole objective of translation.

An awkward line created only to achieve a rhyme can weaken the work.

Where exact rhyme cannot be preserved, consider maintaining other musical or structural qualities.

### 6.4 Repetition

Repetition may convey emphasis, musicality, emotional intensity, or a structural pattern.

Do not remove repeated words or phrases simply because they appear redundant in English.

Before modifying repetition, determine its literary function.

### 6.5 Line Breaks

Line breaks can influence emphasis, ambiguity, rhythm, and meaning.

Preserve them where they are significant to the original form.

Where a translation requires different line breaks, avoid creating an artificial structure that changes the reading unnecessarily.

## 7. Language-Specific Considerations

Different writing systems and literary traditions require different translational approaches.

The following considerations provide a general framework rather than an exhaustive account of every language.

### 7.1 Urdu

Urdu poetry frequently uses compressed expression, layered vocabulary, Persian and Arabic influences, idiomatic constructions, and culturally significant imagery.

Translation should pay attention to:

- Original Urdu script.
- Ghazal and nazm conventions.
- The distinction between sher, misra, and broader poetic structure.
- Persian and Arabic-derived vocabulary.
- Idiomatic expressions.
- Multiple meanings of frequently used literary words.
- Cultural and philosophical references.
- The musicality and cadence of the original.

Where a word has no satisfactory single English equivalent, an explanatory note may be preferable to an inaccurate substitution.

Urdu should retain its right-to-left presentation and appropriate typographic treatment.

### 7.2 Hindi

Hindi literary expressions may involve Sanskrit-derived vocabulary, Perso-Arabic influences, regional idioms, and different registers of everyday and literary language.

Translation should preserve distinctions in tone, register, and cultural meaning rather than assuming that every familiar expression has a single standard equivalent.

Where historical or regional usage affects interpretation, explain it appropriately.

### 7.3 Marathi

Marathi literature contains distinctive idioms, regional expressions, social references, devotional traditions, and varied literary registers.

Translation should preserve the character of the original rather than converting regionally meaningful expressions into generic Hindi or English concepts.

Where a word carries a specific cultural or historical association, retain that nuance where reasonably possible.

### 7.4 Kannada

Kannada has a rich literary history, with classical, medieval, devotional, folk, and modern traditions.

Translation should recognise differences in historical language, literary register, poetic conventions, and regional usage.

Expressions rooted in specific cultural or philosophical traditions should not be stripped of their context.

### 7.5 Tamil

Tamil literary traditions include a substantial classical inheritance alongside diverse modern, devotional, philosophical, and regional forms.

Translation should be attentive to historical layers, poetic conventions, grammatical structure, and culturally significant expressions.

A contemporary English equivalent should not automatically replace an older expression without considering its historical meaning.

### 7.6 Malayalam

Malayalam literature combines distinctive Dravidian linguistic structures with historical influences from other languages and literary traditions.

Translation should pay attention to idiom, register, compound expressions, regional usage, and the emotional character of the source.

The translation should avoid making culturally specific expressions sound artificially generic.

### 7.7 Telugu

Telugu literary traditions include classical and modern poetry, devotional writing, folk expression, and varied regional forms.

Translation should consider poetic structure, idiom, sound, cultural context, and the differences between literary and everyday registers.

Where a formal feature cannot be retained naturally in English, explain the relevant choice when it affects interpretation.

### 7.8 Other Languages

For languages not specifically addressed above, apply the same foundational standards:

- Work from a reliable original source.
- Identify the relevant literary form.
- Consult appropriate linguistic resources.
- Respect regional and historical context.
- Preserve meaningful ambiguity.
- Acknowledge translation limitations.
- Identify translators and source publications accurately.

## 8. Song Lyrics and Musical Expressions

Song lyrics require attention to the relationship between language, music, and performance.

Where a song is discussed, distinguish between:

- Original lyricist.
- Composer.
- Singer or performer.
- Music arranger.
- Producer.
- Translator.
- Adapter.

The performer of a song should not automatically be identified as its lyricist.

### 8.1 Lyrics and Musical Context

A lyric may depend on rhythm, melody, repetition, rhyme, and performance.

Translation should not assume that the written words communicate every dimension of the original musical expression.

Where a translation is intended primarily for reading, identify it as a literary translation rather than implying that it is a singable adaptation.

### 8.2 Singable Adaptations

A singable adaptation may prioritise syllable count, stress, musical phrasing, and performance requirements.

It may therefore depart considerably from a close literary translation.

Identify such material as an adaptation and attribute it correctly.

### 8.3 Reproduction Rights

Song lyrics and their translations may be protected independently of recordings or performances.

The availability of lyrics on an online platform does not automatically grant permission to reproduce them.

Copyright and permission requirements must be assessed before including substantial lyric excerpts or translations.

## 9. Translator's Notes

Translator's notes may be used when a meaningful aspect of the original cannot be represented adequately through the translation alone.

Useful reasons include:

- A word has several significant meanings.
- An idiom has no direct English equivalent.
- A cultural reference requires explanation.
- A historical expression has changed in meaning.
- A literary form affects interpretation.
- A translation decision involves a meaningful compromise.
- The source contains textual variants.

Translator's notes should be concise and relevant.

They should clarify the translation rather than become a separate essay that unnecessarily interrupts the literary experience.

## 10. Independent Translation Workflow

When preparing an original translation for Verse & Variable, follow this sequence.

### Stage 1 — Source Establishment

- Identify the original work.
- Locate a reliable text or recording.
- Verify the original language and creator.
- Establish the edition or version.
- Review textual variants.

### Stage 2 — Initial Reading

- Read the complete work, not just the selected line.
- Identify its literary form.
- Understand the surrounding context.
- Identify important expressions and ambiguities.
- Note relevant grammatical and cultural features.

### Stage 3 — Preliminary Translation

Prepare an initial translation that prioritises meaning and contextual accuracy.

Avoid forcing rhyme or elaborate phrasing at this stage.

### Stage 4 — Literary Refinement

Review the translation for:

- Natural English expression.
- Tone and emotional register.
- Metaphor and imagery.
- Rhythm and cadence.
- Repetition and structure.
- Preservation of ambiguity.
- Faithfulness to the original.

### Stage 5 — Comparison Against the Original

Compare the completed translation line by line with the original.

Check for:

- Added meanings.
- Missing meanings.
- Altered imagery.
- Unsupported interpretation.
- Incorrect idiomatic equivalents.
- Distorted grammatical relationships.
- Unintended changes in tone.

### Stage 6 — Linguistic Verification

Consult suitable dictionaries, grammars, scholarly references, and language resources where necessary.

Do not rely exclusively on a machine translation or a single dictionary definition.

### Stage 7 — Rights and Attribution Review

Establish whether the translation is original, adapted, or reproduced from an existing publication.

Assess the applicable rights and permission requirements.

### Stage 8 — Editorial Review

Check the translation alongside the original and the proposed commentary.

The translation should support the article's interpretation without introducing claims that the original cannot sustain.

## 11. Machine Translation and AI-Assisted Translation

Machine translation and AI tools may assist the translation process, but their output must not be accepted automatically.

Such tools may help with:

- Preliminary vocabulary exploration.
- Alternative phrasings.
- Grammatical comparisons.
- Initial translation drafts.
- Identification of possible ambiguities.
- Comparison of different translation approaches.

However, machine-generated output may introduce:

- Incorrect idiomatic meanings.
- Invented context.
- Loss of cultural nuance.
- Flattened poetic expression.
- Incorrect authorial or historical information.
- Unsupported interpretations.
- Fabricated linguistic explanations.

Any machine-assisted translation intended for publication must be checked against the original text and reliable linguistic resources.

AI-generated translations should not be described as independently verified human translations merely because they have been edited.

Where meaningful AI assistance is used, disclose it when required by the publication's editorial or disclosure policy and applicable circumstances.

## 12. Translation Attribution

Every published translation should have a clear status.

Suggested descriptions include:

**For an independently prepared translation:**

> English translation prepared for Verse & Variable.

**For an existing published translation:**

> English translation by [Translator], from [Publication or Edition].

**For an adapted translation:**

> Adapted English translation based on [Original Work / Translation Source].

**For a translation with uncertain attribution:**

> Translator attribution could not be independently verified.

Use these descriptions only when they accurately reflect the translation's origin.

Do not invent a translator's identity or imply permission that has not been obtained.

## 13. Copyright and Translation Rights

Translations may have their own copyright protection even when the original work is in the public domain.

The publication should separately assess:

- Rights in the original literary work.
- Rights in an existing translation.
- Rights in an adaptation.
- Rights in associated illustrations or recordings.
- Applicable permission and attribution requirements.

Preparing a new translation does not automatically authorise unrestricted reproduction of a protected original work.

Likewise, public availability of a translation does not establish permission to reproduce it.

Refer to `COPYRIGHT.md` and `ASSET_CREDITS.md` when assessing the applicable requirements.

## 14. Translation Quality Review

Before publication, consider the following questions.

### Source verification

- [ ] Is the original text verified?
- [ ] Is the relevant edition or version identifiable?
- [ ] Is the source language correctly identified?
- [ ] Is the author or attribution properly established?

### Translation accuracy

- [ ] Does the translation communicate the original meaning?
- [ ] Are important words interpreted in context?
- [ ] Are metaphors and symbols handled carefully?
- [ ] Is the emotional register preserved?
- [ ] Are ambiguities retained where meaningful?
- [ ] Have unsupported meanings been introduced?
- [ ] Have important expressions been omitted?

### Literary quality

- [ ] Is the English natural and readable?
- [ ] Is the original literary character respected?
- [ ] Are rhythm and structure considered?
- [ ] Is repetition preserved where meaningful?
- [ ] Are line breaks appropriate?
- [ ] Has rhyme been prioritised at the expense of meaning?

### Attribution and rights

- [ ] Is the translator correctly identified?
- [ ] Is the translation original, published, or adapted?
- [ ] Are source references available?
- [ ] Have applicable rights been considered?
- [ ] Are required permissions and acknowledgements in place?

### Final presentation

- [ ] Is the original script presented correctly?
- [ ] Is the translation clearly distinguished from the original?
- [ ] Is transliteration identified separately?
- [ ] Are translator's notes included where necessary?
- [ ] Is the article consistent with the editorial policy?

## 15. Translation and Literary Commentary

Translation and interpretation should remain distinct editorial activities.

A translation communicates the literary expression in another language.

The accompanying commentary explores its meaning, significance, imagery, philosophical dimensions, and possible interdisciplinary connections.

The commentary should not be used to conceal weaknesses in a translation.

Similarly, a particular translation choice should not be presented as the only possible interpretation of the original work.

Where a translation decision materially shapes the commentary, that relationship should be recognised.

## 16. Continuing Development

Translation is a continuing intellectual practice.

New editions, linguistic research, alternative translations, and reader feedback may reveal improvements to previously published work.

Verse & Variable remains open to thoughtful translation corrections and alternative readings supported by appropriate linguistic and literary reasoning.

Substantial corrections should be reflected in the article and, where appropriate, the changelog.

The purpose of these guidelines is not to impose one universal translation method. It is to encourage translations that remain faithful, thoughtful, transparent, and respectful of the literary traditions from which they emerge.
