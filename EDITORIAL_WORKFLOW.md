## 1. Purpose

This document defines the editorial workflow followed when selecting, researching, preparing, reviewing, and publishing literary explorations on Verse & Variable.

The workflow is intended to maintain consistency across languages, literary traditions, individual articles, and weekly collections.

Every published exploration should demonstrate:

- Respect for the original literary expression.
- Reliable identification and attribution of the work.
- Careful attention to language and cultural context.
- Independent and substantial commentary.
- Intellectual honesty in interdisciplinary connections.
- Responsible treatment of copyright and third-party material.
- Clarity, accessibility, and editorial consistency.

The workflow is a quality framework, not a requirement to force every literary work into the same interpretative pattern.

## 2. The Publishing Model

The intended publishing format consists of three independent literary explorations per week, accompanied by a weekly collection page.

Each exploration receives:

- Its own permanent article page.
- Original literary material, where appropriately available.
- Language and attribution information.
- Transliteration and translation, where useful.
- Four substantial paragraphs of original English commentary.
- Relevant references and contextual notes.

The weekly collection page brings together the three individual explorations while allowing each article to remain independently accessible.

Publication frequency must never take priority over verification, quality, or responsible use of source material.

## 3. Stage One — Literary Selection

### 3.1 Identify a Worthwhile Work

The editorial process begins with the selection of a literary expression that offers meaningful scope for exploration.

Potential material includes:

- Urdu poetry, ghazals, nazms, and shayari.
- Hindi poetry and literature.
- Marathi literary expressions.
- Kannada poetry and literature.
- Tamil literary traditions.
- Malayalam poetry and literature.
- Telugu literary expressions.
- Other Indian and South Asian works.
- International literature where its inclusion is meaningful.

Selection should be based on literary merit, depth of expression, cultural significance, philosophical substance, emotional resonance, linguistic richness, or other defensible editorial considerations.

Popularity alone is not sufficient justification.

### 3.2 Identify the Literary Form

Determine the nature of the selected material.

For example:

- Ghazal.
- Nazm.
- Rubai.
- Couplet or sher.
- Song lyric.
- Folk expression.
- Free verse.
- Classical poetry.
- Prose passage.
- Philosophical writing.
- Dramatic or spoken-word expression.

Understanding the literary form helps establish the appropriate way to present and interpret the work.

### 3.3 Selection Notes

Before proceeding, record:

- The proposed work.
- Its original language and script.
- The known creator or attributed source.
- Its literary form.
- The reason for selecting it.
- Any likely verification or copyright concerns.
- The initial editorial direction.

A selected work may be reconsidered or replaced when reliable information or editorial suitability cannot be established.

## 4. Stage Two — Source Verification

### 4.1 Establish the Original Source

Identify reliable sources for the work wherever possible.

Potential sources include:

- Authoritative literary publications.
- Published collections and books.
- Original recordings or performances.
- Reputable archives and libraries.
- Publisher records.
- Scholarly publications.
- Reliable interviews and historical documentation.

Search-engine snippets, social media quotations, and unattributed quotation websites should not be treated as sufficient evidence of authorship or authenticity.

### 4.2 Verify the Text

Where the original material is available, compare the selected text against a reliable source.

Pay particular attention to:

- Exact wording.
- Original script.
- Verse or stanza order.
- Punctuation where meaningful.
- Missing or additional lines.
- Variant readings.
- Dialect and regional vocabulary.
- Differences between written and performed versions.

Where multiple versions exist, the publication should identify the version being discussed rather than silently combining them.

### 4.3 Verify Attribution

Identify the appropriate creator and distinguish between different creative roles.

For songs and musical works, these may include:

- Lyricist.
- Composer.
- Singer or performer.
- Music arranger.
- Translator.
- Adapter.
- Producer.

For literary works, verify the writer or poet using credible bibliographic or historical references.

Where authorship remains uncertain, attribution should be qualified rather than asserted as fact.

### 4.4 Document Sources

Maintain a record of the sources used to establish the text, attribution, historical context, and relevant background.

Prefer original or authoritative sources over repeated secondary claims.

Where sources disagree, preserve the distinction and explain relevant uncertainty when it affects the article.

## 5. Stage Three — Rights and Reproduction Review

Before including the original material in an article, determine whether the proposed reproduction is appropriate.

Check:

- Whether the work is in the public domain.
- Whether a specific open licence applies.
- Whether permission has been obtained.
- Whether a limited quotation is justified and lawful.
- Whether the proposed use is consistent with applicable copyright requirements.
- Whether translations, recordings, illustrations, or other adaptations have separate rights.

A work being publicly accessible does not automatically mean that it can be reproduced without permission.

Where the rights position is unclear, seek permission, reduce the reproduction to an appropriate lawful quotation, link to the original source, or reconsider the proposed presentation.

The presence of a translation online does not establish permission to reproduce that translation.

Follow `COPYRIGHT.md` and `ASSET_CREDITS.md` when assessing the relevant material.

## 6. Stage Four — Language and Cultural Research

### 6.1 Understand the Original Expression

The original language is the primary reference for interpreting a literary work.

Research should consider:

- Vocabulary and idiom.
- Grammar and syntax.
- Metaphorical and symbolic usage.
- Historical meanings.
- Regional or dialectal variations.
- Cultural and philosophical references.
- Literary conventions.
- Social and historical context.

Where a word carries several meanings, those possibilities should not be reduced to an arbitrary single English equivalent.

### 6.2 Transliteration

Where transliteration is useful, establish a readable and consistent representation of the original script.

The transliteration should preserve the identity of the original words as far as practical.

It should not be presented as a substitute for the original script.

### 6.3 Translation

Translation should aim to preserve meaning, tone, imagery, and emotional character while acknowledging that complete equivalence between languages is rarely possible.

Where an existing published translation is used, verify its creator and reproduction rights.

Where a translation is prepared specifically for the article, identify it as the publication's own translation or adaptation as appropriate.

Do not silently modify a published translation and present it as the original translator's work.

### 6.4 Cultural Sensitivity

Interpretation should account for the cultural and historical setting of the work.

Avoid:

- Removing expressions from their cultural context.
- Treating one regional interpretation as universal.
- Imposing contemporary meanings without explanation.
- Flattening religious, philosophical, or historical references.
- Treating linguistic differences as errors.
- Presenting personal interpretation as established historical fact.

Cultural context should deepen the reading rather than become unnecessary background material.

## 7. Stage Five — Literary Interpretation

### 7.1 Identify the Central Expression

Before writing commentary, identify what makes the selected work worth exploring.

This may involve:

- Emotional tension.
- An image or metaphor.
- A philosophical question.
- A contradiction or paradox.
- A distinctive use of language.
- A relationship between individual experience and society.
- A question of memory, identity, time, nature, love, loss, or existence.

The exploration should emerge from the literary material itself.

### 7.2 Develop an Independent Reading

The commentary should express a considered interpretative perspective.

It should not merely paraphrase the original text or repeat familiar explanations from secondary sources.

The writing should demonstrate:

- Close engagement with the words.
- Attention to imagery and expression.
- Thoughtful interpretation.
- Intellectual curiosity.
- Awareness of ambiguity.
- A coherent line of reflection.

Where multiple interpretations are possible, the commentary should remain open to that complexity.

### 7.3 Preserve Literary Priority

Literature remains the starting point and central subject of every exploration.

A scientific idea, philosophical concept, or technical analogy may enrich the discussion, but it should never displace the literary work merely to create an appearance of interdisciplinarity.

Some articles may have no scientific connection at all.

That is a legitimate and valuable editorial outcome.

## 8. Stage Six — Scientific and Interdisciplinary Research

This stage is undertaken only when a meaningful connection emerges from the selected work.

### 8.1 Identify a Genuine Connection

Potential scientific connections may involve:

- Nonlinear dynamics.
- Oscillations and vibrations.
- Wave propagation.
- Nonlinear elasticity.
- Harmonic generation and scattering.
- Hysteresis.
- Contact mechanics.
- Wave mixing.
- Metamaterials.
- Time-dependent physical systems.
- Computational modelling.
- Mathematics and physical acoustics.
- Other relevant scientific disciplines.

A connection should be based on a defensible conceptual relationship rather than superficial similarity in terminology.

### 8.2 Establish the Scientific Basis

Before incorporating a scientific explanation, identify its evidential and theoretical status.

Distinguish carefully between:

1. An established scientific principle.
2. A mathematically defined relationship.
3. A theoretical prediction.
4. A proposed physical mechanism.
5. A numerical observation.
6. An experimental result.
7. A literary or philosophical analogy.
8. An exploratory hypothesis.

These categories must not be treated as interchangeable.

### 8.3 Verify Scientific Claims

Use relevant academic literature, authoritative textbooks, original research, and reliable technical documentation.

Where appropriate, verify:

- Definitions.
- Governing equations.
- Assumptions.
- Physical mechanisms.
- Boundary conditions.
- Model limitations.
- Experimental evidence.
- Relevant citations.

A scientific statement should not be included merely because it sounds sophisticated.

### 8.4 Protect the Difference Between Metaphor and Mechanism

A literary image may resemble a scientific phenomenon without representing the same process.

For example, a metaphor involving resonance does not establish mechanical resonance. A literary description of instability does not establish a nonlinear dynamical instability.

Such comparisons may still be intellectually valuable, provided their interpretative nature is explicit.

### 8.5 Decide Whether to Include the Connection

The scientific dimension should be retained only when it contributes something substantial to the exploration.

If the connection requires excessive explanation, weak assumptions, or unsupported speculation, it should be removed or clearly presented as an open question.

Literary depth takes precedence over technical decoration.

## 9. Stage Seven — Writing the Four-Paragraph Commentary

Every individual literary exploration should contain exactly four substantial paragraphs of original English commentary.

These four paragraphs form the principal interpretative body of the article.

Titles, headings, original quotations, transliteration, translations, attribution, references, captions, and editorial notes are supplementary elements and do not count as part of the four main paragraphs.

### Paragraph One — Entry into the Literary Work

Introduce the literary expression through a thoughtful reading of its language, emotional character, imagery, or central idea.

Establish why the work invites attention.

The paragraph should develop a natural connection with the reader without relying on exaggerated praise or promotional language.

### Paragraph Two — Deeper Interpretation

Explore the underlying ideas, tensions, symbols, relationships, contradictions, or philosophical dimensions.

Engage with the subtleties of the original expression.

Where relevant, consider historical and cultural context.

The paragraph should deepen rather than simply repeat the opening interpretation.

### Paragraph Three — Interdisciplinary Reflection

Where a meaningful connection exists, develop it carefully.

This may involve scientific principles, mathematical ideas, physical analogies, or broader philosophical perspectives.

Clearly distinguish literary interpretation from scientific explanation.

Where no meaningful scientific connection exists, this paragraph may develop another literary, cultural, philosophical, or human dimension of the work.

A scientific connection must never be inserted merely to satisfy a structural expectation.

### Paragraph Four — Synthesis and Reflection

Bring the exploration to a considered conclusion.

Reflect on what makes the literary expression enduring, meaningful, or open to further thought.

Avoid reducing the work to a moral lesson, a simplistic summary, or an artificial concluding statement.

The final paragraph should leave the reader with a genuine intellectual or emotional point of reflection.

### Writing Standards

The four paragraphs should be:

- Substantial rather than artificially lengthened.
- Original rather than assembled from secondary commentary.
- Written in a mature, natural, reflective voice.
- Intellectually coherent.
- Grammatically and stylistically polished.
- Sensitive to cultural and linguistic context.
- Free from promotional exaggeration.
- Supported by appropriate references where factual claims require them.

The paragraphs should read as a connected essay rather than four disconnected observations.

## 10. Stage Eight — Attribution and References

Before publication, prepare the article's attribution and source information.

Depending on the material, this may include:

- Work title.
- Original language.
- Author or poet.
- Lyricist.
- Composer.
- Performer.
- Translator.
- Original publication or recording.
- Publication date, where known.
- Relevant edition or version.
- Bibliographic references.
- Stable source links.
- Copyright or permission information.

Unverified biographical information should not be added merely to make an article appear more complete.

Distinguish the original source of a work from the secondary sources used to interpret it.

Scientific references should support the particular claims they accompany.

All references should be relevant, identifiable, and as stable as reasonably possible.

## 11. Stage Nine — Article Preparation

Prepare the article using the established entry layout and metadata conventions.

Where applicable, include:

1. Article title.
2. Week identifier and entry number.
3. Original language and language metadata.
4. Original literary expression.
5. Transliteration.
6. English translation.
7. Verified authorship and attribution.
8. Four substantial commentary paragraphs.
9. Relevant scientific or contextual notes.
10. Source references.
11. Article navigation and related links.

Language-specific direction, script rendering, typography, and metadata should be checked before publication.

Article frontmatter should remain consistent with the fields expected by the Jekyll entry layout.

The article should also have a meaningful title, description, permanent URL, and appropriate search and social metadata.

## 12. Stage Ten — Editorial Review

Every article should undergo a final review before publication.

### Literary review

- Does the commentary engage meaningfully with the original expression?
- Is the interpretation coherent?
- Does it avoid unsupported claims about authorial intention?
- Is the cultural context respected?
- Is the original literary form preserved?

### Attribution review

- Is the author or creator correctly identified?
- Are musical and translation roles distinguished?
- Are source references reliable?
- Are uncertainties acknowledged?

### Language review

- Is the original text accurate?
- Is transliteration consistent?
- Is the translation faithful to the intended meaning?
- Are regional expressions handled appropriately?
- Is the English commentary natural and polished?

### Scientific review

- Are scientific claims technically accurate?
- Are assumptions and limitations clear?
- Are hypotheses distinguished from established results?
- Is the scientific connection genuinely useful?
- Are references adequate?

### Structure and quality review

- Are there exactly four principal commentary paragraphs?
- Does each paragraph contribute something distinct?
- Is the article free from repetition?
- Does the article follow the publication's editorial policy?
- Are copyright and attribution requirements addressed?

An article that fails an important verification or editorial requirement should be revised before publication.

## 13. Stage Eleven — Accessibility and Technical Quality Assurance

Before publishing, verify the article's presentation and functionality.

Check:

- Correct language metadata.
- Appropriate text direction.
- Urdu right-to-left presentation, where applicable.
- Readable regional-script rendering.
- Font fallbacks.
- Heading hierarchy.
- Image alternatives.
- Link descriptions.
- Keyboard navigation.
- Visible focus indicators.
- Text contrast.
- Responsive layouts.
- Reduced-motion behaviour.
- Article navigation.
- Browser rendering.

For scientific illustrations, verify that meaningful information is not available only through visual interpretation.

The article should remain understandable without depending on animated effects or decorative elements.

## 14. Stage Twelve — Publication

Once the article passes editorial and technical review:

1. Add the entry to the Jekyll collection.
2. Verify its frontmatter and permanent URL.
3. Confirm that the correct layout is applied.
4. Check the generated article page.
5. Verify navigation to the previous and next entries, where applicable.
6. Confirm its inclusion in the appropriate weekly collection.
7. Check the homepage and archive presentation.
8. Verify relevant metadata and search visibility settings.
9. Publish through the repository's established GitHub Pages workflow.
10. Inspect the live article after deployment.

The weekly collection page should include the three intended entries and present them in the correct order.

A failed build or incorrectly rendered page should be corrected before treating the article as successfully published.

## 15. Stage Thirteen — Post-Publication Review

Publication does not end editorial responsibility.

After an article becomes publicly available, remain receptive to:

- Attribution corrections.
- Translation improvements.
- Historical and cultural clarifications.
- Scientific corrections.
- Broken references.
- Accessibility feedback.
- Copyright concerns.
- Technical presentation problems.

Significant corrections should be reflected in the article and, where appropriate, recorded in `CHANGELOG.md`.

Changes should preserve a clear distinction between correcting an error and revising an interpretative perspective.

## 16. Weekly Collection Preparation

After the individual explorations are prepared, create or update the corresponding weekly collection.

The collection should:

- Identify the week clearly.
- Present its three individual explorations.
- Link to each permanent article page.
- Preserve the correct sequence.
- Provide a brief introduction where useful.
- Avoid replacing the full independent articles with abbreviated summaries.
- Maintain consistent metadata and navigation.

Each exploration should remain independently discoverable through its permanent page.

The weekly collection provides coherence without making the individual articles dependent on one another.

## 17. Editorial Decisions and Exceptions

Not every selected work will progress through every stage.

A work may be:

- Replaced because attribution cannot be established.
- Delayed because the original text requires verification.
- Published with a qualified attribution.
- Presented through a shorter lawful quotation rather than extensive reproduction.
- Published without transliteration or translation where these would add little value.
- Explored entirely through literary and philosophical interpretation.
- Held back when reliable scientific or historical evidence is unavailable.

Editorial judgement should take precedence over mechanical completion of a checklist.

The purpose of this workflow is to support thoughtful publishing, not to create unnecessary procedural barriers.

## 18. Responsibility and Continuous Improvement

The editorial workflow should evolve as the archive expands and its requirements become clearer.

The publication may revise its research practices, technical checks, attribution conventions, and article structure when experience demonstrates a meaningful improvement.

Any substantial change should remain consistent with:

- `EDITORIAL_POLICY.md`
- `CONTRIBUTING.md`
- `COPYRIGHT.md`
- `PRIVACY.md`
- `ACCESSIBILITY.md`
- `DISCLAIMER.md`
- `ASSET_CREDITS.md`
- `ROADMAP.md`

The final responsibility is to publish work that is thoughtful, traceable, culturally respectful, scientifically honest, and worthy of a lasting literary archive.
