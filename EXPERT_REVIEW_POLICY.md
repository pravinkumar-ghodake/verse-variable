## 1. Purpose

This document establishes the principles and procedures for obtaining specialist feedback and expert review for material published by Verse & Variable.

The publication explores literature, poetry, languages, cultural traditions, philosophy, mathematics, mechanics, physics, and other scientific disciplines.

Some articles may involve questions that require knowledge beyond the immediate editorial preparation process.

Examples include:

- Historical authorship and literary attribution.
- Complex linguistic and grammatical interpretations.
- Translation of culturally specific expressions.
- Historical and philosophical claims.
- Mathematical derivations.
- Nonlinear physical mechanisms.
- Computational modelling.
- Experimental interpretation.
- Material behaviour and engineering applications.

Specialist input can improve the accuracy and intellectual quality of such material.

This policy defines how expert contributions may be sought, evaluated, acknowledged, and distinguished from formal peer review.

## 2. Fundamental Principles

### 2.1 Appropriate Expertise

The person reviewing a particular claim should have relevant knowledge or experience in the subject concerned.

A general professional title does not automatically establish expertise in every topic discussed by an article.

### 2.2 Evidence-Based Evaluation

Expert comments should be considered alongside the underlying evidence, original sources, mathematical formulations, research methods, and other relevant material.

The status of a reviewer alone does not establish that a claim is correct.

### 2.3 Editorial Independence

Specialist reviewers may offer recommendations, corrections, alternative interpretations, or methodological observations.

Final editorial decisions remain the responsibility of the publication.

### 2.4 Transparency

Where specialist input makes a material contribution to an article, that contribution should be acknowledged or disclosed appropriately, subject to the reviewer's preferences and any confidentiality arrangements.

### 2.5 No Implied Endorsement

Reviewing an article does not automatically imply endorsement of its entire contents, conclusions, editorial direction, or other material.

### 2.6 Respect for Different Disciplines

Literature, linguistics, history, philosophy, mathematics, and science have different methods of inquiry.

A review should respect the standards and limitations of the specific discipline being examined.

### 2.7 No Unsupported Claims of Peer Review

The publication must not describe an article as formally peer-reviewed merely because a knowledgeable person has read it or provided comments.

## 3. Scope of Expert Review

Specialist input may be sought for:

- Literary analysis.
- Language and grammar.
- Original-script verification.
- Transliteration.
- Translation.
- Historical context.
- Author identification.
- Cultural interpretation.
- Philosophical concepts.
- Mathematical reasoning.
- Physical and mechanical explanations.
- Computational methods.
- Scientific evidence.
- Engineering applications.
- Research citations.
- Technical and accessibility issues.

Not every article requires external specialist review.

The decision to seek review should depend on the complexity, importance, uncertainty, and potential consequences of the material.

## 4. Types of Review

The publication distinguishes between several forms of review.

### 4.1 Editorial Review

Editorial review evaluates whether an article satisfies the publication's standards.

It may examine:

- Structure.
- Clarity.
- Literary engagement.
- Originality of commentary.
- Attribution.
- Translation presentation.
- References.
- Editorial consistency.
- Accessibility.

Editorial review does not automatically establish specialist validation.

### 4.2 Specialist Consultation

Specialist consultation involves seeking advice from a person with relevant subject knowledge.

It may involve:

- Checking a technical explanation.
- Evaluating a historical claim.
- Clarifying a linguistic ambiguity.
- Reviewing a translation decision.
- Identifying an incorrect mathematical formulation.
- Suggesting relevant scholarly sources.

This is a form of expert input, not necessarily formal peer review.

### 4.3 Technical Review

Technical review examines a particular technical component.

Examples include:

- Mathematical derivations.
- Numerical implementation.
- Computational code.
- Software architecture.
- Scientific visualisations.
- Web accessibility.
- Structured-data implementation.

A technical reviewer should evaluate only the aspects within their relevant competence.

### 4.4 External Review

External review refers to feedback from a person who is not directly responsible for preparing the article.

An external reviewer may be a researcher, academic, professional, translator, writer, independent specialist, or other knowledgeable individual.

External review is not automatically equivalent to academic peer review.

### 4.5 Formal Peer Review

Formal academic peer review generally involves an identifiable scholarly evaluation process with defined review criteria, independent reviewers, and an appropriate editorial or institutional framework.

Verse & Variable should not claim formal peer-reviewed publication status unless a genuine formal review process has been conducted and documented.

A private email exchange, informal conversation, professional suggestion, or individual technical opinion does not by itself constitute formal peer review.

## 5. When Specialist Review May Be Appropriate

Expert review should be considered when an article contains material claims whose accuracy is important and cannot be established adequately through ordinary editorial verification.

### 5.1 Literary and Historical Review

Specialist input may be useful when:

- Authorship is disputed.
- Multiple editions contain significant variations.
- A historical attribution is uncertain.
- A literary form requires specialist interpretation.
- A cultural expression has complex historical associations.
- An article discusses a specialised literary movement.

### 5.2 Linguistic Review

Specialist input may be appropriate when:

- A passage depends on subtle grammatical distinctions.
- Regional vocabulary affects the interpretation.
- A translation contains significant ambiguity.
- A historical linguistic form is being discussed.
- An original script contains difficult or disputed readings.
- A transliteration decision requires specialist knowledge.

### 5.3 Philosophical Review

A specialist may be consulted when an article:

- Attributes a philosophical position to a particular thinker.
- Compares distinct philosophical schools.
- Interprets a specialised philosophical text.
- Makes historical claims about the development of an idea.
- Uses terminology whose meaning varies across traditions.

### 5.4 Scientific Review

Specialist input may be appropriate when an article contains:

- Nonlinear constitutive relationships.
- Complex mathematical derivations.
- Technical claims about wave propagation.
- Nonlinear ultrasonic mechanisms.
- Harmonic generation or wave mixing explanations.
- Hysteresis or contact-nonlinearity models.
- Metamaterial design principles.
- Time-modulated physical systems.
- Nonlinear cloaking concepts.
- Scientific simulation results.
- Experimental interpretations.

### 5.5 Computational Review

Computational or numerical review may be useful when an article relies on:

- Finite element formulations.
- Numerical convergence claims.
- Computational implementation.
- Solver behaviour.
- Parameter estimation.
- Bayesian inversion.
- Optimisation.
- Scientific software.
- Reproducibility claims.

### 5.6 No Mandatory Review for Every Article

The publication does not require every literary exploration to undergo external review.

An article based primarily on literary interpretation and supported by adequately verified sources may be prepared through the ordinary editorial workflow.

Specialist review should be sought when it meaningfully improves reliability, rather than being imposed as a ceremonial requirement.

## 6. Reviewer Expertise and Suitability

The suitability of a reviewer should be evaluated in relation to the specific subject being examined.

### 6.1 Relevant Qualifications and Experience

Potential indicators of expertise may include:

- Relevant academic qualifications.
- Research publications.
- Professional experience.
- Established scholarly work.
- Subject-specific technical contributions.
- Language and translation experience.
- Historical or archival research.
- Documented practical expertise.
- Demonstrable knowledge of the relevant research methods.

Formal academic qualifications may be useful, but they are not the only possible evidence of expertise.

Independent researchers, experienced translators, archivists, practitioners, and other specialists may provide valuable contributions.

### 6.2 Verify Relevant Background

Where appropriate, verify the reviewer's professional or scholarly background through:

- Institutional profiles.
- Published research.
- Professional portfolios.
- Official biographies.
- Academic identifiers.
- Relevant publications.
- Other reliable public records.

Do not invent qualifications, appointments, affiliations, or professional credentials.

### 6.3 Scope of Competence

A reviewer's expertise should be matched to the particular claims being reviewed.

For example:

| Subject requiring review | Relevant expertise |
|---|---|
| Urdu grammatical interpretation | Urdu linguistics or relevant language expertise |
| Marathi historical expression | Marathi language and literary or historical scholarship |
| Classical Tamil poetic form | Tamil literary scholarship |
| Malayalam translation | Relevant Malayalam linguistic and translation expertise |
| Philosophical attribution | Relevant philosophical scholarship |
| Nonlinear elastic constitutive model | Nonlinear solid mechanics |
| Harmonic generation | Relevant nonlinear acoustics or wave physics |
| Finite element implementation | Computational mechanics or numerical methods |
| Time-dependent metamaterial behaviour | Relevant wave physics and metamaterial research |

These examples are indicative rather than exclusive.

### 6.4 Avoid Overgeneralising Expertise

A person qualified in one scientific discipline should not automatically be described as having reviewed the entire scientific content of an article.

Likewise, a translator's linguistic expertise does not automatically validate historical or scientific claims.

## 7. Selecting Reviewers

Reviewers should be selected according to relevance, competence, independence, and availability.

### 7.1 Selection Criteria

Consider:

- Relevant subject knowledge.
- Experience with the specific problem.
- Familiarity with the relevant language or research tradition.
- Ability to evaluate the evidence.
- Independence from the particular claim.
- Potential conflicts of interest.
- Willingness to provide a considered assessment.

### 7.2 Independent Expertise

Where possible, seek reviewers who can evaluate the material independently rather than relying only on people who already support the article's conclusions.

A meaningful review should permit disagreement.

### 7.3 Multiple Disciplines

Some interdisciplinary articles may require more than one specialist.

For example, an article connecting Urdu poetry with nonlinear wave physics may benefit from separate linguistic and scientific feedback.

One reviewer should not be expected to validate both fields merely because the article connects them.

### 7.4 Reviewer Availability

Specialist review is voluntary unless a separate formal arrangement has been established.

No individual should be presented as a reviewer merely because they were contacted or invited.

## 8. Conflicts of Interest and Reviewer Independence

A reviewer should be able to offer an independent assessment.

Relevant relationships should be considered before review begins.

### 8.1 Potential Conflicts

These may include:

- Financial interests.
- Direct professional competition.
- Close personal relationships.
- Institutional or employment relationships.
- Prior involvement in the disputed work.
- Ownership or authorship of the material under review.
- Public commitments to a particular conclusion.
- Other relationships that could materially affect impartiality.

### 8.2 Disclosure by Reviewers

Where relevant, ask a reviewer to identify relationships that could influence or appear to influence the review.

### 8.3 Managing Conflicts

Possible responses include:

- Proceeding with disclosure.
- Limiting the scope of the review.
- Seeking additional independent input.
- Replacing the reviewer.
- Declining to rely on the assessment.

### 8.4 Disagreement Does Not Automatically Establish Bias

A reviewer may disagree strongly with an article without having a conflict of interest.

Disagreement should be evaluated on the basis of the arguments, evidence, and relevant circumstances.

Do not describe a reviewer as biased merely because their conclusion differs from the publication's preferred interpretation.

## 9. Review Scope and Questions

A review request should identify the specific material requiring evaluation.

A reviewer should not be expected to assess an entire interdisciplinary article when only one technical question has been submitted.

### 9.1 Recommended Review Request

A review request may contain:

- Article title.
- Relevant article section.
- Subject requiring review.
- Specific claim or interpretation.
- Supporting references.
- Particular questions.
- Requested scope.
- Known uncertainties.
- Relevant time or technical constraints, where appropriate.

### 9.2 Specific Questions

Examples include:

**Literary review**

- Does the interpretation accurately reflect the relevant literary form?
- Is the historical context appropriate?
- Are the cited textual variants correctly identified?

**Linguistic review**

- Is the original expression accurately transcribed?
- Does the proposed translation preserve the intended meaning?
- Are important ambiguities or regional expressions overlooked?

**Scientific review**

- Is the governing physical principle correctly described?
- Are the mathematical assumptions appropriate?
- Does the cited study support the stated conclusion?
- Is the distinction between numerical prediction and experimental evidence maintained?

**Computational review**

- Is the numerical formulation appropriate?
- Are convergence and validation claims justified?
- Are the computational limitations adequately described?

### 9.3 Bounded Review

Where possible, define what the reviewer is not being asked to evaluate.

For example, a reviewer assessing a harmonic-generation explanation is not automatically being asked to validate the article's literary interpretation.

## 10. Evidence and Supporting Material

Reviewers should receive the material necessary for the requested assessment.

Depending on the subject, this may include:

- Relevant article sections.
- Original literary text.
- Translation and transliteration.
- Source references.
- Mathematical equations.
- Figures.
- Model assumptions.
- Experimental information.
- Computational methodology.
- Relevant standards or technical documentation.

### 10.1 Source Transparency

Do not ask a reviewer to validate a claim without providing the relevant source or enough context to identify it.

### 10.2 Research Status

Clearly identify whether a scientific statement comes from:

- Published peer-reviewed research.
- A preprint.
- A theoretical formulation.
- A numerical simulation.
- An experiment.
- An unpublished research proposal.
- A literary analogy.

### 10.3 Unavailable Evidence

Where essential evidence cannot be supplied, identify the limitation.

A reviewer should not be asked to certify a result that cannot reasonably be examined.

## 11. Confidentiality and Review Material

Review material should be handled responsibly.

Depending on the circumstances, reviewers may receive unpublished drafts or research information that is not intended for public circulation.

### 11.1 Confidential Material

Do not distribute:

- Private correspondence.
- Restricted research documents.
- Unpublished third-party manuscripts.
- Confidential institutional material.
- Personal information.
- Proprietary datasets.

unless there is appropriate authority and a legitimate reason for sharing it.

### 11.2 Reviewer Confidentiality

Where confidential review has been agreed, do not publicly reproduce the reviewer's comments or identify the reviewer without an appropriate basis or permission.

### 11.3 No Automatic Public Disclosure

An invitation to review does not automatically authorise public use of the reviewer's name, institutional affiliation, comments, or endorsement.

### 11.4 Secure Handling

Use appropriate communication and document-sharing practices.

Avoid sending sensitive material through unsuitable public channels.

Refer to `PRIVACY.md` and `SECURITY.md`.

## 12. Reviewer Contributions and Acknowledgement

Specialists may contribute in different ways.

Acknowledgement should reflect the actual contribution rather than exaggerating the nature of the involvement.

### 12.1 Informal Consultation

A specialist who provides a brief clarification may be acknowledged, with their permission, where the contribution is material.

### 12.2 Substantive Technical Review

A person who evaluates a specific mathematical, scientific, linguistic, or historical claim may receive more explicit recognition where appropriate.

### 12.3 Substantial Intellectual Contribution

If a reviewer makes a substantial original intellectual contribution that materially changes the article, consider whether a different form of credit is appropriate.

Such a contribution should not automatically be described as ordinary feedback.

### 12.4 Reviewer Consent

Before publicly naming a reviewer, obtain their agreement unless public identification is already clearly authorised.

### 12.5 Acknowledgement Language

Suitable wording may include:

> The author thanks [Name] for specialist comments on [specific subject or section]. Responsibility for the article's final interpretation and conclusions remains with its author.

Use this statement only when it accurately describes the contribution and the reviewer's preferences.

### 12.6 No Implied Endorsement

Acknowledgement does not mean that the reviewer approves every statement, argument, or conclusion in the article.

## 13. Formal Peer Review: Important Distinction

Verse & Variable is an independent literary and intellectual publication.

Its ordinary editorial and specialist consultation processes should not automatically be described as formal academic peer review.

### 13.1 Informal Expert Feedback

The following do not independently establish formal peer-review status:

- A private discussion with a professor.
- Comments from a researcher.
- Email feedback from a specialist.
- A review of one equation.
- A translation suggestion.
- An informal manuscript reading.
- Acknowledgement of expert assistance.

These may be valuable forms of consultation, but should be described accurately.

### 13.2 Formal Peer Review Requirements

If the publication introduces a genuine formal peer-review process in the future, it should establish a separate and documented framework.

Depending on the process, this may include:

- Defined submission criteria.
- Independent reviewers.
- Reviewer selection standards.
- Conflict-of-interest declarations.
- A documented evaluation procedure.
- Review criteria.
- Editorial decision procedures.
- Confidentiality requirements.
- Reviewer acknowledgement practices.
- Record retention.
- Publication status terminology.

The existence of one or more reviews is not sufficient on its own to establish a formal process.

### 13.3 Review Model

Any future formal process should clearly identify whether it is:

- Single-anonymous review.
- Double-anonymous review.
- Open review.
- Another documented review model.

The selected terminology should correspond to the actual procedure.

### 13.4 Public Claims

Do not use statements such as:

- Peer-reviewed article.
- Academically certified.
- Institutionally approved.
- Expert-endorsed.

unless the relevant formal process or relationship genuinely exists.

### 13.5 No Retrospective Certification

An article should not be described as formally peer-reviewed after receiving informal specialist feedback.

A later specialist consultation may be disclosed, but it does not retroactively change the original publication process into formal peer review.

## 14. Review Outcomes

A specialist review may produce different types of recommendations.

| Outcome | Description |
|---|---|
| Accepted | The reviewer considers the relevant claim adequately supported within the requested scope |
| Accepted with qualifications | The claim is reasonable but requires clarification or limitations |
| Revision recommended | Changes are needed before the claim can be retained |
| Additional evidence required | The available material is insufficient |
| Disagreement | The reviewer offers a different defensible interpretation or conclusion |
| Outside scope | The question exceeds the reviewer's relevant expertise |
| Unable to assess | The reviewer cannot provide a reliable assessment using the supplied material |

These descriptions represent possible feedback outcomes, not automated editorial decisions.

## 15. Editorial Decisions Following Review

The reviewer provides advice. The publication makes the editorial decision.

Possible decisions include:

- Retain the original wording.
- Revise the explanation.
- Add a qualification.
- Introduce additional references.
- Remove an unsupported claim.
- Seek another specialist opinion.
- Expand the methodological explanation.
- Delay publication.
- Decline to publish the affected material.

A reviewer's recommendation should be considered carefully, but it does not automatically determine the final article.

### 15.1 Documenting Decisions

Where a substantive review results in an important editorial change, record the relevant decision and its reasoning in an appropriate internal research or editorial record.

Do not unnecessarily publish confidential reviewer correspondence.

### 15.2 Unresolved Disagreement

If credible reviewers disagree, examine the underlying arguments and evidence.

Where the disagreement cannot be resolved, the article may:

- Present the competing interpretations.
- Qualify its conclusion.
- Restrict the scope of the claim.
- Explain the unresolved uncertainty.
- Remove the claim if it cannot be supported adequately.

The publication should not conceal legitimate disagreement merely to produce a more definitive article.

## 16. Specialist Review of Interdisciplinary Articles

Interdisciplinary articles may require more than one kind of expertise.

A literary expression connected with a scientific subject should be evaluated in distinct intellectual dimensions.

### 16.1 Literary Dimension

Consider:

- Original language.
- Textual accuracy.
- Literary form.
- Imagery.
- Historical context.
- Interpretative coherence.

### 16.2 Linguistic Dimension

Consider:

- Grammar.
- Vocabulary.
- Idiomatic meaning.
- Translation.
- Transliteration.
- Ambiguity.
- Regional expression.

### 16.3 Scientific Dimension

Consider:

- Physical principles.
- Mathematical relationships.
- Governing equations.
- Modelling assumptions.
- Research evidence.
- Experimental or numerical limitations.

### 16.4 Connection Between Disciplines

Finally, consider whether the connection itself is defensible.

A specialist in one field may identify the limits of an analogy without being required to validate the other discipline's entire interpretation.

### 16.5 No Forced Scientific Validation

The publication does not require every literary exploration to have a scientific reviewer.

Some articles may be entirely literary, philosophical, cultural, or historical.

The absence of scientific review is not a deficiency when no substantive scientific claim is being made.

## 17. Review of Published Articles

Specialist review may also be sought after publication.

This may happen when:

- New evidence becomes available.
- A reader identifies a technical problem.
- A scientific explanation is disputed.
- A translation is challenged.
- Historical authorship becomes uncertain.
- An article receives substantive criticism.

Post-publication specialist feedback should be distinguished from the original publication process.

## 19. Review Disclosure Statements

Where specialist review or consultation makes a material contribution, an appropriate disclosure may be included.

### 19.1 Specialist Consultation

> **Specialist consultation:** The author consulted [Name] regarding [specific subject]. The consultation informed the preparation of the article.

### 19.2 Technical Review

> **Technical review:** The mathematical formulation in [specific section] was examined by [Name] for technical consistency.

### 19.3 Linguistic Review

> **Linguistic consultation:** [Name] provided feedback on the interpretation and translation of the original-language expression.

### 19.4 Historical Consultation

> **Historical consultation:** The historical context discussed in this article benefited from feedback provided by [Name].

### 19.5 Limited Review Scope

> **Review scope:** Specialist feedback was limited to the [identified section or subject] and should not be interpreted as approval of the entire article.

### 19.6 Non-Peer-Reviewed Editorial Statement

> This article underwent editorial review. It has not been represented as a formally peer-reviewed academic publication.

Use these statements only when they accurately describe the review process and the relevant individual's contribution.

## 20. Review Records and Confidentiality

For substantive specialist review, maintain an appropriate record.

Depending on the circumstances, this may include:

- Article identifier.
- Review date.
- Subject of review.
- Scope of the request.
- Relevant qualifications or experience.
- Conflict disclosures.
- Principal feedback.
- Editorial decision.
- Acknowledgement permission.
- Follow-up corrections.

Review records should be retained only as reasonably necessary.

Do not publish confidential review comments, private contact details, or personal information without appropriate authority.

The publication may preserve an internal record without making the underlying correspondence public.

## 21. Reviewer Recognition

Specialist contributions may be recognised through appropriate editorial acknowledgement.

Recognition should be:

- Accurate.
- Proportionate.
- Voluntary where public identification is involved.
- Consistent with the reviewer's actual contribution.
- Clear about the scope of the assistance.

The publication should not use a reviewer's name to create an exaggerated impression of institutional support, formal peer review, or endorsement.

A public list of contributors or advisers may be considered in the future, but only where the individuals have agreed to the relevant identification and the described relationship remains accurate.

## 24. Future Development

As the archive develops, the publication may consider additional specialist review arrangements.

Potential developments include:

- A voluntary specialist feedback network.
- Language-specific consultation.
- Scientific and technical advisory input.
- Historical and philosophical consultation.
- More structured review records.
- Formal review procedures for selected research-oriented publications.

These are possible future developments, not claims that such a network or formal review system currently exists.

Any future arrangement should clearly define its purpose, reviewer responsibilities, conflicts of interest, confidentiality, recognition, and review status.

## 25. Continuing Commitment

Intellectual independence does not require intellectual isolation.

A serious interdisciplinary publication benefits from criticism, specialist knowledge, alternative interpretations, and careful technical examination.

However, meaningful review depends on asking the right questions of the right people and communicating clearly what has and has not been examined.

Verse & Variable seeks to welcome specialist knowledge while preserving editorial independence, recognising intellectual contributions fairly, and remaining transparent about the limits of every review process.

The objective is not to make every article appear academically certified. It is to make every substantive claim as carefully examined and responsibly presented as its subject requires.
