## 1. Purpose

This document establishes the lifecycle management principles for all content published by Verse & Variable.

The publication is intended to become a growing multilingual literary and intellectual archive. Its content will evolve through research, editorial development, technical maintenance, corrections, and the discovery of additional historical, linguistic, cultural, and scientific information.

A clear content lifecycle helps ensure that published material remains:

- Properly researched and verified.
- Correctly attributed.
- Consistent with editorial standards.
- Legally and ethically responsible.
- Technically accessible.
- Traceable through its publication history.
- Maintained throughout its useful lifetime.

This document establishes how content moves from an initial idea to a published and maintained archive entry.

It also addresses the circumstances in which content may require correction, revision, temporary restriction, replacement, or withdrawal.

## 2. Scope

The content lifecycle applies to:

- Individual literary explorations.
- Weekly collections.
- Original literary commentary.
- Original translations.
- Published translations and adaptations.
- Historical and cultural essays.
- Scientific and interdisciplinary discussions.
- Research-oriented articles.
- Supplementary illustrations and multimedia.
- Informational and editorial pages.
- Public documentation associated with the publication.

Not every content type requires every lifecycle stage.

For example, an informational page may not require literary-source verification, while an article discussing a poem may require extensive linguistic, attribution, and copyright review.

The lifecycle should be adapted to the nature and significance of the material.

## 3. Fundamental Lifecycle Principles

### 3.1 Quality Before Publication Frequency

Content should progress only when it satisfies the requirements appropriate to its subject.

A publication schedule must not justify publishing incomplete, unverified, or misleading material.

### 3.2 Traceability

Important editorial and research decisions should remain traceable.

Where appropriate, records should identify:

- Original source.
- Research references.
- Translation decisions.
- Attribution.
- Editorial revisions.
- Publication date.
- Significant corrections.
- Relevant technical changes.

### 3.3 Preservation of Original Expression

Original literary material should be preserved carefully.

Subsequent revisions to commentary, metadata, or presentation should not accidentally alter the original work.

### 3.4 Transparent Corrections

Material corrections should be documented appropriately.

Readers should not be misled into believing that a substantially corrected article has remained unchanged.

### 3.5 Responsible Withdrawal

Removing an article may sometimes be necessary because of legal, ethical, factual, or other serious concerns.

Withdrawal should be handled deliberately and should not be confused with ordinary editorial revision.

### 3.6 Long-Term Maintainability

The lifecycle should support a publication that can grow over many years without depending on undocumented editorial decisions or fragile technical processes.

## 5. Stage One — Idea and Discovery

The lifecycle begins when a literary work, subject, expression, or intellectual question is identified for possible exploration.

Potential starting points include:

- A poem or poetic couplet.
- A ghazal or nazm.
- A song lyric.
- A philosophical passage.
- A regional literary expression.
- A historical text.
- A cultural or linguistic observation.
- A scientific concept that invites meaningful literary reflection.

An idea is not automatically accepted for publication.

At this stage, the purpose is to identify its potential and establish whether further research is worthwhile.

### 5.1 Initial Research Notes

Where appropriate, record:

- Proposed work or subject.
- Original language.
- Known author or creator.
- Possible literary form.
- Initial source.
- Reason for considering the material.
- Possible interpretative direction.
- Potential verification issues.

### 5.2 Discovery Sources

Discovery may begin through libraries, books, recordings, scholarly publications, archives, interviews, or other resources.

A discovery source should not automatically become the authoritative publication source.

Original material and important claims must be verified independently where appropriate.

## 6. Stage Two — Literary Selection

The selected material undergoes an initial editorial assessment.

Consider:

- Literary significance.
- Linguistic richness.
- Cultural relevance.
- Emotional or philosophical depth.
- Historical importance.
- Originality of the proposed exploration.
- Availability of reliable source material.
- Copyright and permission considerations.

The selection should provide a meaningful basis for independent commentary.

### 6.1 Selection Decisions

Possible outcomes include:

- Accepted for research.
- Retained for future consideration.
- Deferred pending additional information.
- Replaced with a more suitable work.
- Rejected because it does not meet editorial requirements.

A rejected selection need not be treated as a failure.

It may simply be unsuitable for this publication.

## 7. Stage Three — Initial Assessment

Before substantial work begins, assess whether the proposed article can realistically meet the publication's standards.

The initial assessment should consider:

### Literary suitability

Can the original expression be identified and meaningfully explored?

### Source reliability

Are reliable sources available for the original wording and attribution?

### Linguistic feasibility

Can the original language be understood and translated responsibly?

### Rights position

Can the proposed material be reproduced lawfully and appropriately?

### Research requirements

Do the intended historical, cultural, philosophical, or scientific claims have adequate supporting sources?

### Editorial contribution

Will the article contribute something substantive and original to the archive?

Where significant uncertainties remain, identify them before drafting.

## 8. Stage Four — Research and Verification

Once a work has been selected, conduct the research required for its publication.

This stage may involve:

- Original text verification.
- Author identification.
- Literary-form analysis.
- Historical research.
- Linguistic investigation.
- Translation preparation.
- Cultural contextualisation.
- Philosophical research.
- Scientific research.
- Bibliographic documentation.

### 8.1 Source Register

Create and maintain the relevant research record using:

`SOURCE_REGISTER.md`

Record important sources and the information they support.

### 8.2 Research Standards

Apply:

`RESEARCH_STANDARDS.md`

Different types of claims require different kinds of evidence.

Historical claims, linguistic interpretations, scientific results, and literary commentary should not be treated as having identical evidential requirements.

### 8.3 Research Completion

Research should be considered sufficiently complete only when the important claims supporting the proposed article have been checked.

Some interpretative questions may remain open.

Unresolved questions should be identified rather than concealed.

## 9. Stage Five — Rights and Attribution Review

Before preparing the final publication version, review the rights associated with the material.

This includes:

- Original literary work.
- Existing translations.
- Adaptations.
- Lyrics.
- Images.
- Illustrations.
- Audio and video recordings.
- Research figures.
- Other third-party resources.

### 9.1 Attribution

Verify the identities and roles of relevant contributors.

Distinguish between:

- Original author.
- Poet.
- Lyricist.
- Composer.
- Performer.
- Translator.
- Adapter.
- Publisher.

Do not create unsupported attribution claims.

### 9.2 Rights Decision

Possible outcomes include:

- Suitable for publication in the proposed form.
- Suitable with required attribution.
- Suitable after obtaining permission.
- Suitable only with a reduced or modified quotation.
- Suitable through a link to the original source rather than reproduction.
- Not suitable for publication in the proposed form.

See `COPYRIGHT.md` and `ASSET_CREDITS.md`.

## 10. Stage Six — Draft Preparation

The article is prepared using:

`ARTICLE_TEMPLATE.md`

The draft should contain the elements appropriate to the selected work.

These may include:

- Article title.
- Original literary expression.
- Original language identification.
- Transliteration.
- English translation.
- Attribution.
- Relevant contextual information.
- Four substantial paragraphs of original English commentary.
- Scientific or interdisciplinary reflection, where meaningful.
- References.
- Supplementary notes.

### 10.1 Four-Paragraph Requirement

Each individual literary exploration must have exactly four substantial paragraphs of principal English commentary.

The four paragraphs form the central interpretative contribution.

Headings, quotations, translations, metadata, references, and supplementary notes do not count as part of those four paragraphs.

### 10.2 Draft Integrity

The draft should distinguish:

- Verified facts.
- Direct quotations.
- Original commentary.
- Interpretative statements.
- Scientific explanations.
- Speculative observations.

Do not present sourced text as original writing.

## 11. Stage Seven — Editorial Review

The completed draft undergoes editorial review.

The review should consider:

### Literary quality

- Is the original expression accurately represented?
- Does the interpretation engage meaningfully with the work?
- Does the commentary retain its independent voice?
- Is the cultural context respected?

### Linguistic quality

- Is the original language correctly identified?
- Is the transliteration appropriate?
- Is the translation defensible?
- Are ambiguities handled carefully?
- Are language-specific conventions respected?

### Intellectual quality

- Are philosophical interpretations reasonable?
- Are scientific connections meaningful?
- Are assumptions and limitations clear?
- Are established findings distinguished from hypotheses?

### Structural quality

- Are exactly four principal commentary paragraphs present?
- Does the article follow the established template?
- Are repetitions avoided?
- Are headings and supplementary materials appropriate?

### Research integrity

- Are important claims supported?
- Are references accurate?
- Are disputed facts qualified?
- Is attribution correct?

An article that does not meet important editorial standards should be revised before moving to publication approval.

## 12. Stage Eight — Technical and Accessibility Review

The reviewed article must also work correctly within the website.

This includes checking:

- Jekyll frontmatter.
- Collection membership.
- Article layout.
- Permanent URL.
- Weekly identifier.
- Entry number.
- Language metadata.
- Original-script rendering.
- Right-to-left presentation, where applicable.
- Typography and font fallbacks.
- Internal navigation.
- Search and social metadata.
- Structured data, where implemented.
- Responsive layout.
- Keyboard interaction.
- Screen-reader compatibility.
- Image descriptions.
- Reduced-motion behaviour.

### 12.1 Build Verification

Check that the Jekyll build completes successfully.

A successful source-file commit is not sufficient evidence that the generated website is correct.

Inspect the rendered page after deployment.

### 12.2 Weekly Collection Integration

Verify that the article appears in the intended weekly collection.

The publication's intended weekly structure consists of three independent literary explorations.

All associated entries should use the correct shared `week_id` and distinct `entry_number` values.

## 13. Stage Nine — Publication Approval

An article should be approved for publication only when its editorial, research, rights, and technical requirements have been addressed.

### 13.1 Publication Readiness

Before release, verify that:

- The original work is sufficiently verified.
- Attribution is accurate or appropriately qualified.
- Translation status is clear.
- Copyright requirements are addressed.
- Commentary is complete.
- Scientific claims are supported where applicable.
- References are functional.
- The page renders correctly.
- The article's permanent URL is established.

### 13.2 Approval Decision

Possible outcomes include:

- Approved for publication.
- Approved subject to specified corrections.
- Returned for further review.
- Deferred.
- Withdrawn from consideration.

Approval should not be interpreted as a guarantee that an article can never require correction.

Editorial knowledge can develop after publication.

## 14. Stage Ten — Publication and Release

Approved content is added to the appropriate Jekyll collection.

### 14.1 Individual Article Publication

The entry should be created within:

`_entries/`

The article should contain the approved frontmatter and content.

### 14.2 Weekly Collection Publication

The weekly collection should be created within:

`_weeks/`

Use:

`WEEKLY_COLLECTION_TEMPLATE.md`

Verify that the intended three articles are associated with the correct weekly identifier.

### 14.3 Deployment

After the repository changes are committed:

1. Allow the configured GitHub Pages publishing workflow to process the changes.
2. Confirm that the build and deployment complete successfully.
3. Open the permanent article URL.
4. Check the original text and translations.
5. Inspect article formatting.
6. Verify navigation.
7. Check the weekly collection.
8. Confirm that the page is publicly accessible.

### 14.4 Release Record

The publication should retain the relevant Git commit history.

Significant publication-level changes may also be recorded in:

`CHANGELOG.md`

The original article URL should remain stable after release unless a justified change is necessary.

## 15. Stage Eleven — Post-Publication Monitoring

Published material remains subject to ongoing review.

The publication may receive new information through:

- Reader feedback.
- Academic publications.
- Newly accessible archives.
- Corrected editions.
- Historical discoveries.
- Linguistic research.
- Scientific developments.
- Attribution clarifications.
- Accessibility reports.
- Copyright concerns.

Review material claims when credible new information becomes available.

Not every new interpretation requires a revision.

However, substantive factual errors should not be ignored simply because an article has already been published.

## 16. Article Revision and Version History

Articles may require modification after publication.

Revisions should be classified according to their significance.

### 16.1 Minor Corrections

Examples include:

- Typographical corrections.
- Punctuation adjustments.
- Minor formatting problems.
- Broken links.
- Non-substantive metadata corrections.

These changes may not require a prominent editorial correction notice.

The corresponding source changes should remain available through the repository's version history.

### 16.2 Substantive Corrections

Examples include:

- Incorrect authorship.
- Incorrect quotation.
- Translation errors that change meaning.
- Significant historical inaccuracies.
- Scientific inaccuracies.
- Incorrect claims about research findings.
- Misleading attribution.

Such corrections should be made deliberately and documented appropriately.

Where the correction changes the reader's understanding of the article, a visible correction note may be warranted.

### 16.3 Editorial Revisions

The commentary may occasionally be improved without correcting a factual error.

Examples include:

- Clarifying an argument.
- Improving the explanation of a metaphor.
- Adding relevant context.
- Refining a scientific distinction.
- Expanding a discussion.

A substantial interpretative revision should be distinguishable from a minor language edit when the difference is material to the article.

### 16.4 Revision Dates

Where displayed or represented in metadata, `dateModified` should reflect a genuine meaningful modification.

Do not update article modification dates automatically on every website rebuild.

### 16.5 Version History

The Git repository provides an important record of changes to source files.

Avoid rewriting repository history in ways that unnecessarily destroy the historical record of published material.

## 17. Corrections and Reader Feedback

Reader feedback is an important part of maintaining a reliable archive.

Feedback may concern:

- Literary attribution.
- Original text.
- Translation.
- Transliteration.
- Historical context.
- Scientific explanations.
- Copyright.
- Accessibility.
- Technical presentation.

### 17.1 Reviewing a Correction

When a concern is received:

1. Identify the affected article and passage.
2. Understand the nature of the reported problem.
3. Request supporting information where necessary.
4. Check the original source and relevant references.
5. Assess the significance of the issue.
6. Decide whether a correction or other action is appropriate.
7. Update the content if justified.
8. Record the change where appropriate.

### 17.2 Correction Outcomes

Possible outcomes include:

- Correction accepted.
- Correction partially accepted.
- Additional research required.
- No change required.
- Article temporarily restricted.
- Material removed or replaced.

A correction request should be assessed on its evidence rather than on the identity or prominence of the person submitting it.

### 17.3 Correction Transparency

Do not silently make a major correction that materially changes a published claim when readers would reasonably benefit from knowing about the change.

A concise correction note may explain:

- What was incorrect.
- What has been corrected.
- When the correction occurred.
- Which source or evidence supports the change.

See `EDITORIAL_POLICY.md` and `RESEARCH_STANDARDS.md`.

## 18. Temporary Restriction and Withdrawal

There may be circumstances in which continued public availability of an article requires reconsideration.

Examples include:

- A credible copyright concern.
- Serious attribution problems.
- Materially misleading factual claims.
- Unintended disclosure of private information.
- A significant security issue.
- A legal requirement.
- A serious ethical concern.

### 18.1 Temporary Restriction

Where immediate review is necessary, temporarily restricting public access may be appropriate.

The action should be proportionate to the issue.

### 18.2 Withdrawal

Withdrawal is a significant editorial decision.

It should be considered when an article cannot responsibly remain published in its existing or corrected form.

Possible reasons include:

- Reproduction of material without a sustainable rights basis.
- Fundamental source verification failure.
- Serious and unresolvable attribution problems.
- Material that should not have been publicly disclosed.
- Legal or ethical obligations requiring removal.

### 18.3 Preservation of Editorial Records

Where legally and ethically permissible, retain appropriate internal records explaining the withdrawal decision and the publication history.

Do not expose confidential correspondence, personal information, or sensitive material merely to demonstrate that a withdrawal occurred.

### 18.4 Public Notice

Depending on the circumstances, a public notice may explain that material has been withdrawn or revised.

The notice should not reproduce the problematic material or reveal protected information unnecessarily.

### 18.5 Permanent Removal

A withdrawn page may be removed or replaced with an appropriate explanatory page.

Check related links, weekly collections, archive entries, sitemap information, and search metadata.

Do not leave broken navigation or continue presenting withdrawn material as an active article.

## 19. Long-Term Archival Preservation

A major objective of Verse & Variable is to maintain a durable literary archive.

Long-term preservation requires more than keeping public URLs available.

### 19.1 Source Preservation

Retain relevant project source files within the repository, subject to applicable rights and licensing conditions.

These may include:

- Original article Markdown files.
- YAML metadata.
- Weekly collection files.
- Original SVG illustrations.
- Project CSS.
- Project JavaScript.
- Documentation.
- Appropriate supporting assets.

### 19.2 Research Documentation

Maintain relevant research references and source records.

Where appropriate, preserve:

- Bibliographic information.
- Source identifiers.
- Translation decisions.
- Editorial notes.
- Correction records.
- Significant revision history.

Do not retain confidential or restricted information merely for completeness.

### 19.3 Git History

Git history provides a record of repository changes.

Use meaningful commit messages.

Avoid unnecessary rewriting or deletion of history.

### 19.4 Static Content Resilience

Where practical, publish core literary content in standard, portable formats such as Markdown, text, and valid HTML.

Avoid making the long-term readability of an article depend exclusively on a temporary third-party script or service.

### 19.5 External Dependencies

Review external fonts, libraries, scripts, and embedded resources periodically.

Where an external resource becomes unavailable, consider a suitable alternative while preserving the original article's meaning and presentation.

### 19.6 Backup Considerations

A public repository is useful for version control and distribution, but should not be assumed to be the only preservation copy.

Consider maintaining independent backups of important repository content and publication resources.

Backups should be tested periodically.

## 20. Publication Status Vocabulary

For consistent editorial management, the following status vocabulary is recommended.

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `idea` | Initial subject identified |
| `selected` | Work accepted for further consideration |
| `researching` | Verification and research underway |
| `drafting` | Article being prepared |
| `in-review` | Editorial assessment underway |
| `revision-required` | Changes required before approval |
| `approved` | Editorial and relevant technical checks completed |
| `scheduled` | Prepared for a planned release |
| `published` | Publicly available |
| `under-correction` | Published article undergoing substantive review or correction |
| `archived` | Retained but no longer actively promoted or maintained as current content |
| `withdrawn` | Removed or replaced because of a significant issue |

### 20.1 Status Is Not Automatically a Jekyll Field

These statuses are editorial workflow conventions.

They do not imply that the existing Jekyll templates or layouts already support an automated status-management system.

If statuses are introduced in article frontmatter or an editorial tracking tool, their implementation should be documented and tested separately.

### 20.2 Publication Visibility

Only material approved for public release should appear as published content.

Drafts, internal research notes, and confidential information should not be included accidentally in public collections or generated search indexes.

### 20.3 Archived Versus Withdrawn

These statuses represent different decisions.

An archived article remains part of the publication's historical record.

A withdrawn article has been removed from active publication because continued availability is inappropriate.

Do not treat them as interchangeable.

## 21. Relationship with Weekly Publication Cycles

The publication's intended weekly format consists of three independent literary explorations.

Each article should pass through its own research and editorial process.

The three articles are then brought together through a weekly collection page.

### 21.1 Independent Approval

One article should not automatically be considered ready because the other two are complete.

Each must meet the necessary standards independently.

### 21.2 Weekly Collection Readiness

A weekly collection should be considered ready only when its intended article links and collection metadata have been checked.

### 21.3 Schedule Flexibility

If one exploration requires further verification, the publication should prioritise quality rather than force the article into an artificial deadline.

An incomplete article should not be treated as published merely to maintain a three-article schedule.

## 22. Technical and Administrative Responsibilities

The lifecycle is an editorial framework, but its implementation depends on maintaining the technical publication system.

Relevant responsibilities include:

- Correct repository organisation.
- Valid Jekyll frontmatter.
- Consistent article identifiers.
- Working collection relationships.
- Reliable permanent URLs.
- Correct sitemap output.
- Accurate RSS content.
- Appropriate structured metadata.
- Accessible presentation.
- Reliable backup and version history.

Changes to technical infrastructure should be reflected in relevant documentation and the changelog.

## 23. Lifecycle Review Checklist

Before an individual article is treated as a completed publication item, review its lifecycle.

### Selection and research

- [ ] Literary selection is justified.
- [ ] Original source is identified.
- [ ] Original text is verified.
- [ ] Attribution is checked.
- [ ] Language and cultural context are researched.
- [ ] Important scientific claims are supported where applicable.

### Rights and writing

- [ ] Copyright position has been reviewed.
- [ ] Translation status is identified.
- [ ] Original commentary is complete.
- [ ] Four principal commentary paragraphs are present.
- [ ] Appropriate references are included.

### Approval and publication

- [ ] Editorial review is complete.
- [ ] Technical review is complete.
- [ ] Accessibility has been checked.
- [ ] Metadata is valid.
- [ ] Weekly collection relationship is correct.
- [ ] Public deployment is successful.
- [ ] Permanent URL has been checked.

### Maintenance

- [ ] Publication history is preserved.
- [ ] Significant corrections are documented.
- [ ] Relevant references remain accessible.
- [ ] External dependencies are reviewed where necessary.
- [ ] Backup arrangements are considered.

## 25. Continuing Commitment

A literary archive should not be regarded as a collection of pages that become irrelevant once published.

Literary texts may acquire new interpretations. Historical records may be corrected. Translations may improve. Scientific explanations may require refinement. Technical presentation may evolve.

The responsibility of Verse & Variable extends beyond the first publication of an article.

Its objective is to maintain an archive in which original expression is respected, research remains traceable, corrections are handled responsibly, and valuable intellectual material can remain accessible over time.
