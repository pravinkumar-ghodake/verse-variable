Some images in poetry become so familiar that we almost forget how extraordinary they were when first imagined. A mountain of pain melting into a river is one such image. In *Ho Gayi Hai Peer Parvat-Si Pighalni Chahiye*, Dushyant Kumar does not describe suffering as something momentary or easily overcome. It has accumulated, hardened and acquired the immensity of a mountain. Yet the opening declaration refuses to accept that immensity as permanent. The mountain must melt. From the Himalaya, a Ganga must emerge. What makes these lines compelling is not simply their expression of pain, but their insistence that pain must eventually become movement.

I find the relationship between the two images particularly powerful. The mountain represents something that has accumulated over time: burdens, disappointments, inequalities and experiences that seem too substantial to be moved. The river, by contrast, represents transformation. It does not merely remove the mountain; it emerges from it, carrying the possibility of movement into a wider landscape. The choice of the Ganga gives this transformation a distinctly Indian geographical and cultural imagination. The image is at once immense and familiar, drawing upon a landscape deeply embedded in collective memory. What begins as an expression of suffering becomes an image of something that can flow beyond the conditions that produced it.

There is also a difference between expressing dissatisfaction and demanding meaningful change. The ghazal's wider movement makes this distinction important. Its language repeatedly turns towards the structures that sustain a condition, rather than being satisfied with the appearance of unrest. A wall may begin to tremble, but the foundations beneath it must also be disturbed. An uproar may draw attention, but the effort must go further: the situation itself must change. This is where I find the poem's public voice particularly significant. It does not treat protest as an end in itself. It asks what remains after the noise, and whether anything has actually moved.

Perhaps the most enduring thought I take from these lines is that suffering should not become an inheritance we simply learn to carry. There are circumstances that seem immovable precisely because they have remained unquestioned for so long. To imagine their transformation is already to refuse their permanence, but imagination alone is not enough. It must find expression in action, persistence and the willingness to change the foundations rather than merely the appearance of things. A mountain does not become a river because someone wishes it to; yet without the possibility of imagining that transformation, the mountain may remain unquestioned forever. Dushyant Kumar's opening couplet leaves me with that tension between what has accumulated and what must begin to move.
