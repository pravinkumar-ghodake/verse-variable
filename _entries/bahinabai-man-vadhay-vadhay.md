I find something wonderfully familiar in Bahinabai's description of the mind. She does not begin with an abstract idea or a philosophical definition. Instead, she imagines an animal wandering into a standing field, being driven away, only to return again and again. There is affection in this observation, almost a smile of recognition. We may repeatedly tell ourselves to stay focused, to stop thinking about something, or to leave a particular question alone. Yet the mind has its own attractions, and it often finds its way back to whatever has caught its attention.

Then the image opens into something larger. The mind is *mokat* — free, unrestrained — finding paths wherever it looks. Bahinabai compares its movement to waves stirred by the wind. I particularly like this transition: what begins as an animal's stubborn return becomes a landscape of countless possible directions. The mind is not simply wandering without purpose; it is responding to a world that continually offers something new to notice, follow and explore.

I have often felt that curiosity works in much the same way. One question leads to another; a book opens an unexpected path, a conversation leaves behind an unfinished thought, and an unfamiliar subject suddenly becomes impossible to ignore. There are moments when this movement may appear scattered to someone looking from outside. But a life of learning cannot always follow a straight road. Some discoveries begin with the freedom to follow an interest before knowing where it might lead.

What I appreciate most is that Bahinabai does not try to correct this wandering mind. She simply observes it, with all its restlessness and peculiar charm. Perhaps we spend too much of our lives trying to make ourselves fit a single description, a single discipline or an expected path. There is room for commitment, certainly, but there should also be room for wonder. I would not want to lose the part of myself that still wanders towards unfamiliar ideas, finds unexpected paths and remains curious about what lies beyond the familiar. Some minds, perhaps, are happiest when they are allowed to keep discovering.
