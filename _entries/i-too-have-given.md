There is something deeply moving about the simplicity of the declaration *Nēnu saitam*—I too. The speaker does not begin by claiming to have changed the world, led a movement or accomplished something extraordinary. Instead, the contribution is described through a small piece of kindling offered to the world's fire. The image is modest, but its setting is immense. The fire belongs not to one person, but to the world; the offering is only a fragment, yet the speaker insists that this fragment belongs there. I find this relationship between the smallness of an individual act and the magnitude of its destination to be the starting point of the poem's emotional force.

The opening image is followed, in the poem, by other forms of participation: a tear offered to the world's rain and a voice released into the world's roar. These are strikingly different contributions. One is an offering, another an expression of sorrow, and the third an act of speaking. The speaker does not present participation as a single heroic gesture. It can take the form of labour, vulnerability, suffering or simply refusing to remain silent. The repeated declaration *Nēnu saitam* gathers these different experiences into one insistence: I, too, am part of this world, and what happens within it is not separate from me.

What makes the poem particularly compelling is that this assertion does not emerge from a position of invulnerability. The speaker recalls the harshness of the seasons, hunger and the conditions through which an individual must endure. The poem then imagines what would happen if that individual were simply to cease standing within the world. Its images become increasingly expansive, moving towards a vision in which the individual's presence and contribution acquire significance beyond their apparent scale. The final movement, with its images of a white petal in the world's lotus, a string in the universal veena and a flag rising above the world's dwelling, gives the declaration a sense of belonging to something larger. Yet the individual does not disappear into that larger whole; the contribution remains recognisably their own.

Perhaps what stays with me most is the distinction between being merely present in the world and consciously participating in it. We often measure contribution through visible achievements, recognised positions or outcomes that can be counted. Sri Sri's poem offers another way of thinking about it. A contribution need not be enormous to matter; it may be one small act, one expression of suffering, one voice among many. But its significance also depends on the world to which it is offered. The poem reminds me that participation is not simply about being part of a collective—it is about recognising that the world is continually being shaped by the contributions, however different in scale, of those who inhabit it. The words *Nēnu saitam* therefore feel less like a claim to importance than an affirmation of responsibility: I, too, have a part in what this world becomes.
