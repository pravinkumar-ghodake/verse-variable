I read Dushyant Kumar's opening couplet as a quiet encounter between vulnerability and the will to continue. The river moves, the breeze brings a little relief, and a battered boat continues to meet the waves. What touches me is that the boat is not made beautiful by concealing its condition. It is *jarjar*—worn, weakened, perhaps carrying the marks of many difficult crossings. Yet it is still there, moving with the river. The couplet does not promise an easy passage or celebrate an effortless victory. It gives dignity to the much quieter act of continuing when circumstances have already taken something from us.

As the ghazal unfolds, this small scene gradually becomes a larger reflection on the ways hope survives. Dushyant turns to a lamp whose wick, though soaked in oil, still waits for a spark; to human sorrow that may remain unheard yet somehow finds its song; and to the evening that has covered an entire city without preventing the road from reaching dawn. These images seem to belong to different worlds, but they share a delicate possibility: something can remain alive even when its strength is difficult to see. I find particular tenderness in the idea that pain, too, can sing. It need not find perfect words or an audience to possess its own expression.

The river returns later in the ghazal, apparently resting in an open plain, yet quietly conversing with the stones hidden along its course. I love this image because it changes the way we look at stillness. A river may appear almost motionless from a distance, while its current continues to find its way around stones and through small passages. Dushyant gives that quiet movement a voice. What seems silent is not necessarily empty; what appears to have stopped may still be carrying on a conversation with the world around it. Perhaps people are not so different. Much of what sustains us happens away from view, in thoughts we cannot easily express and in small adjustments that help us continue.

By the final image, the ghazal has travelled far beyond the battered boat with which it began. Its repeated *to hai* no longer sounds like a simple reassurance; it becomes a gentle insistence that something remains—a breeze, a flame waiting to be lit, a voice within pain, a road towards morning, a river still speaking, and a sky wide enough to hold everything beneath it. The poem does not ask us to deny hardship or pretend that a damaged boat is unharmed. It asks us to notice what hardship has not entirely taken away. That, to me, is the quiet generosity of these lines: hope is not always the certainty that things will improve; sometimes it is the recognition that life, despite everything, has not fallen silent.
