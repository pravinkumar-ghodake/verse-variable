Dushyant Kumar begins with a boat that is already worn by its journey. The river does not become gentler for its sake, nor do the waves disappear. Even the arrival of a cool breeze offers only a moment of relief. Yet the boat continues to meet the water's movement. I find something deeply human in the expression *naav jarjar hi sahi*—the boat may be battered, but its condition does not tell the whole story of its journey. Its persistence is not a declaration of invincibility. It is an acknowledgement that vulnerability and the will to continue can occupy the same vessel.

The wider ghazal makes this persistence more tender and complicated. A faintly burning wick, a voice emerging through pain, the distant suggestion of dawn: these are not images of a world suddenly made whole. They are small continuities within an uncertain world. The repeated *to hai* carries a quiet insistence, as though the speaker is gathering evidence that something worth holding on to still exists. I am particularly drawn to the way the poem refuses to separate hope from its difficult surroundings. Like a light that remains visible without illuminating the entire night, hope need not resolve every uncertainty to make a difference to the person moving through it.

There is a subtle connection here with wave–structure interaction. A boat does not encounter a wave as an isolated, unchanging object confronting an external disturbance. Its response emerges from a coupled relationship between the moving water and the vessel itself. Its inertia, buoyancy, geometry and condition all influence how it moves under the same passing wave. It may heave, roll or pitch, and these motions are not simply signs of weakness; they are part of how a floating body responds to its environment. I find this a useful way of approaching Dushyant's image: being affected by what surrounds us does not mean that we have surrendered our capacity to respond.

Nor does every encounter leave the boat unchanged. Repeated loading can accumulate damage, alter structural integrity and change the response to subsequent waves. In nonlinear mechanics, the relationship between an applied force and the resulting response is not always proportional; the present condition of a system matters. Human experience, too, cannot be understood as though every new difficulty meets us exactly as we were before. We carry the memory of earlier encounters, and our capacity to respond changes with them. Yet the poem does not demand that the boat emerge untouched. Its quiet affirmation is more moving than that: the river continues, the waves remain, and the boat still meets them. Perhaps endurance is not the ability to resist every disturbance, but to retain some measure of direction while being changed by the journey.
