Dushyant Kumar's opening couplet holds two contrasting conditions together: a river that continues to move and a boat already worn by its journey. The breeze arrives, but the boat is still confronted by waves. Its condition is not concealed or romanticised; it is *jarjar*, battered and fragile. Yet it continues to meet the water's motion. I find the strength of this image in its refusal to confuse persistence with invulnerability. The boat is not described as overcoming the river or escaping its current. It remains within the moving water, exposed to its forces, and continues its passage despite its condition.

The wider ghazal gives this persistence a more complex meaning. A faintly burning wick, human pain that finds a voice, a road through darkness towards dawn, and a river that continues its quiet conversation with stones: each image preserves something that might otherwise seem lost. The recurring affirmation *to hai* gives these images their cadence. It does not promise that every difficulty will disappear; it insists that something remains, however diminished or difficult to perceive. The boat is therefore not merely an emblem of optimism. It belongs to a larger landscape of uncertainty in which hope survives alongside exhaustion, silence and vulnerability.

From the perspective of fluid mechanics, the encounter between a river and a floating craft is more than a meeting between two visible objects. A river current represents a mean flow, whereas surface waves introduce time-dependent free-surface disturbances and oscillatory fluid motion. A floating body experiences the resulting hydrodynamic pressures and forces, responding through movements such as heave, roll and pitch. Its response depends on its geometry, mass distribution, buoyancy, inertia and hydrodynamic damping, as well as the conditions under which waves encounter it. The boat is neither a passive observer nor an independent object: its motion is coupled to the surrounding fluid. This is the essential character of wave–structure interaction.

Linear hydrodynamic models provide useful approximations for many small-amplitude conditions, but finite wave amplitudes and stronger interactions can make the response nonlinear. Wave steepness, changing wetted geometry, nonlinear pressure distributions and large-amplitude body motions can all influence the loads and resulting motion. Research into nonlinear seakeeping and hydroelasticity examines precisely these coupled effects in floating structures. I do not read Dushyant's boat as a technical description of a vessel, nor suggest that the poet anticipated such theories. Rather, the image invites a question that connects the mechanics of floating bodies with the human experience of endurance: does continuing a journey require the absence of opposing forces, or the capacity to keep responding to them?
