I find something deeply moving in Dushyant Kumar's opening image of a battered boat continuing to meet the waves. The cool breeze brings a little comfort, but it does not repair the boat or make the river any less demanding. The word *jarjar* stays with me because it acknowledges a condition that cannot be hidden: the boat has been worn down. Perhaps that is what makes its persistence so human. There are times when we continue not because we feel strong, hopeful or certain of where we are going, but because something within us is not yet ready to give up. We may carry the marks of everything we have endured, and still find ourselves facing another day. Dushyant does not make this struggle look glorious. He simply allows the battered boat to remain afloat.

The next image takes that feeling somewhere even more tender. A lamp has oil and a wick, but someone must still find a spark to bring it to life. The poet turns towards his friends and asks them to look for one. I find great warmth in that small word, *dosto*. Hope here is not presented as something every person must manufacture alone. Sometimes we need another person's presence, encouragement or even a few words of kindness to help us find what we thought we had lost. The ghazal then moves towards a ruined place where a wildflower grows and where human pain, though unable to speak, still manages to sing. There is a particular sadness in that image: some of our deepest suffering never becomes a story we can tell completely. It remains hidden in us, finding expression in fragments, in music, in silence and in the smallest gestures.

The evening then settles over the entire city, almost like a blanket covering everything familiar. I read this as one of the poem's most honest moments. Darkness does not disappear simply because we wish for morning, and there are journeys in life when even the next step is difficult to see. Yet Dushyant quietly reminds us that the road through darkness still leads towards dawn. Later, the river appears in an open, almost silent landscape, finding its way among the stones and speaking to them from their shelter. That image feels unexpectedly intimate to me. Not every meaningful conversation happens in the open; not every feeling needs to be heard by the world. Sometimes we find our own ways of speaking, healing and continuing, even when nobody else notices.

By the end, I return to the repeated words *to hai*. Each time they appear, they seem to gather something that hardship has not completely taken away: a little breeze, a wick waiting for a spark, a pain that can still sing, a road that leads beyond the darkness, a river that has not forgotten how to speak. The poem does not promise that every wounded person will recover everything they have lost. Nor does it suggest that hope makes suffering disappear. Its generosity lies elsewhere. It reminds me that a life cannot be measured only by what it has achieved, what it has lost or how easily it has travelled. There may still be tenderness in us, room for another person, a small reason to begin again and an inner sky wider than the difficulties surrounding us. Sometimes that is enough to keep us moving.
