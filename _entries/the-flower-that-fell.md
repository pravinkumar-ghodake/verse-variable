There is something arresting about the way this poem begins. The poet encounters a flower that has fallen, and instead of passing over it as something that has simply withered, he pauses to address it. He remembers the flower as it once stood, almost like a queen in splendour, and places that former beauty beside its present condition. The contrast is immediate, but it does not feel like a detached observation of nature. There is tenderness in the address, as though the fallen flower still deserves the attention and dignity it possessed when it was in full bloom.

What makes this image so compelling is that the flower's life is not reduced to the moment of its fall. It has a past. It was nurtured, surrounded by leaves, moved by the breeze, and shaped by the changing seasons. Its beauty was once part of a larger world of growth and attention. By remembering those earlier stages, Asan allows us to see the flower not merely as an object lying on the ground, but as a life that has passed through different conditions. The fallen state is only one part of its story, even if it is the part that first catches the eye.

I find the poem's treatment of impermanence especially interesting because it begins with something so delicate and particular. We often speak about the passage of time in broad philosophical terms, but here the thought emerges through the visible fate of a single flower. Its former position, its beauty, and the attention it once received all become part of the reflection. There is also a quiet question about how we value things: are we attentive only when something is flourishing, admired, and useful to us? What remains of that attention when its outward beauty has faded? The flower invites us to look beyond the moment of admiration.

Perhaps that is why the image continues to stay with me. The fallen flower is not simply a reminder that life ends; it is an invitation to consider the whole movement of existence, including the stages we tend to overlook. Growth, beauty, decline, and disappearance are not separate worlds but parts of one unfolding experience. Asan finds a profound subject in an ordinary sight, and in doing so reminds me that even what appears to have lost its former splendour can still hold an immense depth of meaning.
