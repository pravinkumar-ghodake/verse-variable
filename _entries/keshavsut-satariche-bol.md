The poem begins with a night in which darkness seems to have entered not only the streets but the speaker's heart. He walks without direction, absorbed in his own disappointments, barely noticing even the stars above him. Then, from a nearby window, comes the sound of a sitar. What surprises me is that he does not immediately welcome it. The music irritates him; his sadness is so deeply rooted that even something beautiful seems unwelcome. He becomes angry with the unseen musician, clenches his fist and wishes the instrument would simply be broken. I find this moment remarkably honest. Sometimes, when the heart is burdened, we do not know how to receive kindness, beauty or even the possibility of happiness. We may turn away from precisely those things that might help us find ourselves again.

And yet, after walking only a few more steps, the speaker turns back. He does not fully understand why. Something in those recurring notes has held his attention, and he finds himself seated nearby, listening with his head resting in his hands. Gradually, the music seems to reach a place that ordinary words could not. His eyes fill with tears, and then comes one of the poem's most tender moments: he imagines someone placing a hand upon his shoulder and gently asking why he carries so much sorrow. The reassurance that follows feels almost like a conversation with someone who understands him without demanding an explanation. I find this deeply human. We do not always need someone to solve our difficulties; sometimes we need only to feel that our sadness has been noticed, that we are not entirely alone in carrying it.

As the music changes, the speaker wipes his eyes and looks towards the sky. The stars that he had not noticed earlier now appear in their countless brightness. The line *tam alp, dyuti bahu* carries a beautiful change of perspective: darkness is little, light is abundant. Nothing in the night has necessarily changed, but something within the person looking at it has. The sitar's notes gradually open into a feeling of beauty, love and an extraordinary sense of belonging. The speaker imagines the whole world becoming beautiful, as though heaven itself were leaning down to touch the earth. For a moment, the distinctions that usually divide experience seem to disappear. Music becomes more than something heard from a distant window; it becomes a way of feeling connected to everything around him. The repeated *दिड दा* returns throughout this journey, almost like a familiar thread guiding him from agitation towards wonder.

Eventually, the music grows quiet. The earth, the night, the wind, the stars and, most importantly, the speaker's heart seem to have found stillness. He returns home peacefully and falls asleep, yet the sitar's notes continue in his dreams. I find this ending more beautiful than a sudden declaration that all sorrow has vanished. The night has not been erased, nor has the poem explained away the disappointments with which the speaker began. Instead, it shows how an unexpected encounter with beauty can alter the way we carry what hurts us. Perhaps each of us has known such moments: a piece of music, a familiar voice, a book or a kindness arriving at a time when we least expect it. We may not remember every difficulty surrounding that moment, but we remember how it made us feel. Keshavsut leaves me with that quiet understanding: sometimes, without changing the circumstances of our lives, something can change the way our hearts experience them.
