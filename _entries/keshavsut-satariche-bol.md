Keshavsut's poem begins in a state of inward heaviness. The speaker walks through a dark night, preoccupied by sorrow, when the recurring sound of a sitar reaches him through a window. At first, the music irritates him; he even wishes the instrument would be broken. Yet, after a few steps, he turns back. The sound draws him into stillness, and he sits listening, gradually losing awareness of his surroundings. What changes is not the night or the circumstances behind his distress, but the direction of his attention. Keshavsut gives this inward transformation a physical sequence: walking, turning, stopping, listening and finally becoming absorbed.

The recurring syllables <span lang="mr"><em>दिड दा, दिड दा</em></span> are central to this progression. They are not merely an imitation of instrumental sound; their repetition accompanies successive changes in the speaker's emotional state. What initially seems intrusive becomes compelling, then consoling, and eventually a source of profound quiet. The music appears to speak when ordinary language cannot. A voice of reassurance emerges within the listening experience, and the speaker begins to perceive the night and its stars differently. Even after returning home, the rhythm continues in his dreams. The poem is therefore not simply about the beauty of a melody, but about how sustained listening can transform the experience of distress.

The sitar also offers a particularly rich perspective through physical acoustics. When a string is plucked, it supports several vibrational modes, producing a spectrum of partials rather than a single pure frequency. Their relative amplitudes and evolving envelopes contribute to the instrument's distinctive timbre. A sitar string, however, does not behave like an ideal string with perfectly fixed endpoints. Its curved bridge constrains the vibrating string, influencing its motion and the force transmitted to the resonating body. The bridge and resonator help shape the sound radiated into the surrounding air. The resulting acoustic field is therefore governed by the interaction of string vibration, structural response and sound radiation.

There is an especially interesting nonlinear dimension here. In a 2012 physical model of the sitar string, Sadjad Siddiq examined how interaction with the bridge constrains the string's motion and produces amplitude-dependent damping. The effective vibrating length changes through contact, while dispersion causes different frequency components to propagate differently along the string. Their combined influence helps explain the complex spectral behaviour of the sitar. This is not merely a metaphorical connection: nonlinear string dynamics and contact interaction are actual parts of the instrument's sound production. I do not suggest that Keshavsut anticipated these theories. Rather, his poem captures something that physical descriptions alone cannot convey: the passage from an external vibration to an intimate human experience. Its final quiet is not an absence of sound, but the peace that remains after listening.
