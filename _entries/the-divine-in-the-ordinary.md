  In the crow's feathers,
  O Nandalala,
  I see the darkness of your own hue,
  O Nandalala.

  In all the trees that I behold,
  O Nandalala,
  I see the greenness of your own hue,
  O Nandalala.

  In every sound that reaches me,
  O Nandalala,
  it is your music that I hear,
  O Nandalala.

  If I place my finger in the fire,
  O Nandalala,
  the joy of touching you
  is what I seem to feel,
  O Nandalala.

What I find beautiful about this poem is where its vision begins. It does not begin with a grand description of a temple, a sacred landscape, or some extraordinary manifestation. It begins with a crow. An ordinary bird, often overlooked in our everyday surroundings, becomes the first place in which the poet recognises Nandalala. The movement is remarkably simple: he looks at the crow's feathers and sees the dark hue of the divine. Then he looks at the trees and recognises that same presence in their green. The familiar world has not changed; what has changed is the way it is being seen.

There is something deeply moving in this way of looking. We often separate the sacred from the ordinary, as though the two belong to different worlds. Bharati seems to dissolve that distance. The crow does not need to become a different bird, nor do the trees need to acquire some extraordinary appearance. They are already worthy of attention. The poet's devotion transforms the act of looking itself, allowing the divine to be recognised in things that are always around us. I particularly like that the poem gives such importance to a crow rather than choosing an image that might conventionally be considered beautiful. It makes the experience of recognition feel open and immediate.

The poem then moves beyond sight. In every sound, the poet hears Nandalala's music. This is a lovely progression: first the colours of the world, then its sounds, and finally the sensation of touch. The last stanza is especially striking. The image of placing a finger in fire introduces an intensity that is quite different from the gentleness of looking at a bird or listening to a sound. Devotion here is no longer simply an act of observing beauty; it becomes an experience that seems to reach the body itself. The divine is not confined to what is pleasant or comfortable.

I am left thinking about how much of our experience depends on the quality of our attention. We pass the same trees, hear the same sounds, and encounter the same creatures every day, often without pausing to notice them. Bharati invites us to look again, but not merely to observe more carefully. He offers a way of experiencing the world in which the familiar can become meaningful without losing its ordinary character. Perhaps that is one of the quiet strengths of this poem: it does not ask the world to become extraordinary. It asks us to recognise what may already be present within it.
