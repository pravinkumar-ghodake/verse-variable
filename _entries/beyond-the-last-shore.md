There is something striking about the voice that speaks through this poem. It does not ask the sea to become calm, nor does it demand that the world make the journey easier. Instead, it confronts the immensity of the unknown with an equally immense sense of purpose. The closing declaration, *Anant amuchi dhyeyāsaktī, anant an āśā*—boundless is our devotion to the quest, and boundless our hope—does not describe an achievement already secured. It expresses a determination that refuses to measure its possibilities by the limits of what has already been reached.

I find the final image particularly compelling: *kinārā tulā pāmarālā*—the shore is yours, O insignificant one. The shore, ordinarily a place of arrival, safety and rest, becomes a boundary that the speaker refuses to accept as the ultimate destination. There is a quiet reversal here. The sea may appear immeasurable, the storm may seem overwhelming, and the horizon may continually recede; yet the speaker locates limitation not in the distance ahead but in the willingness to stop. The contrast between the boundlessness of aspiration and the smallness attributed to the shore gives the closing lines their defiant force.

The poem speaks through the imagined voice of Columbus and his sailors, making it possible to read it as a dramatic monologue rather than a straightforward account of historical events. That distinction matters. The historical voyages associated with Columbus cannot be separated from the violence of European colonisation and its consequences for Indigenous peoples. The poem's imaginative celebration of exploration therefore deserves a historically aware reading. Its poetic energy can be considered on its own terms without turning its central figure into an uncomplicated historical hero.

What stays with me is the idea that a person's horizon need not be determined by the circumstances immediately surrounding them. To pursue something beyond the familiar is not necessarily to know where the journey will end; sometimes it is simply to refuse to let uncertainty become a reason for surrender. Yet aspiration also carries responsibility: the desire to move beyond one's own boundaries should not become a justification for denying the lives, histories or boundaries of others. Perhaps that is where I find the most lasting question in this poem—not merely how far we can travel, but what kind of human imagination accompanies us when we do.
