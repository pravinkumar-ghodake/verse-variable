The word *aniketana* is what stays with me. It is not an ordinary invitation to leave one's home, nor simply a call to travel beyond familiar surroundings. It suggests something more inward: a consciousness that does not allow itself to be confined by a fixed address, an inherited identity, or a single way of understanding the world. The poem addresses the human spirit directly, as though the first journey must begin within oneself.

I find this idea particularly compelling because we often build our sense of belonging around the very boundaries that eventually restrict us. A language, a community, a belief, an institution, or even an intellectual achievement can become a place of comfort. There is nothing inherently wrong with belonging to these worlds. The difficulty begins when we mistake one of them for the entirety of existence. Kuvempu's invocation seems to ask whether the mind can remain open even while it belongs somewhere.

There is also a quiet distinction between freedom and restlessness here. To become *aniketana* need not mean rejecting every attachment or refusing every form of belonging. It may instead mean remaining receptive to what lies beyond the boundaries of one's present understanding. A mind that can question itself, encounter unfamiliar ideas, and recognise the humanity of others need not be homeless in the emotional sense. It can carry its sense of belonging without turning it into a wall.

What I take from this invocation is an invitation to remain intellectually and humanly unfinished. There is always more to learn, more to understand, and more of the world to encounter. Perhaps the spirit becomes truly expansive not when it finds a final destination, but when it no longer needs to believe that one destination contains everything.
